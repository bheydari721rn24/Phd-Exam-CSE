## Summary: the complete reasoning chain

A projection problem starts with a target, a feasible space and a metric. For a finite-dimensional subspace with a positive-definite inner product, the target has a unique decomposition into a feasible component and a component perpendicular to every feasible direction. The first component is the unique closest point. This single certificate derives the matrix formula, its invariance under a basis change, symmetry in an orthonormal coordinate system, idempotence, contraction and the perpendicular energy identity.

Generator coordinates are auxiliary. Repeating columns changes the coefficient descriptions without changing the subspace or its physical closest point. Independent columns permit a Gram inverse; dependent columns require a basis selection or a correctly defined pseudoinverse. Affine constraints require feasibility and translation. Weighted and complex formulas require the corresponding metric and adjoint. Products, sums and differences require additional geometric hypotheses. A successful calculation should end by verifying the geometric certificate in the same ambient space and metric that defined the question.

## Examination decisions and traps, with their reasons

### Identify the object before selecting a formula

1. **State the ambient space.** If generators form an $m\times n$ matrix, the projected vector has $m$ entries and a generator coefficient vector has $n$. A candidate formula with the wrong output dimension is invalid before any arithmetic is attempted.

2. **State the metric.** Ordinary Euclidean geometry uses transpose; complex Euclidean geometry uses conjugate transpose; a declared positive-definite metric inserts its matrix in the inner products. A diagram of ordinary coordinate angles cannot alone certify weighted perpendicularity.

3. **Distinguish a subspace from an affine space.** A linear subspace contains zero. An affine space may not. Its closest-point map generally has a nonzero constant term and is therefore affine rather than linear.

4. **Test feasibility of multiple affine constraints.** A dependent consistent system still has a closest point. An inconsistent system defines an empty feasible set. A least-squares surrogate for incompatible equations is a different problem and must be named as such.

5. **Separate generators from a basis.** A spanning list may contain zeros or dependencies. Orthogonal projection remains unique, but the full generator Gram matrix may have no inverse. Select independent original columns before using the inverse formula.

6. **Use row reduction only to identify column indices.** Original pivot columns span the physical column space. Pivot columns copied from the reduced matrix usually span a transformed space. A perfectly symmetric idempotent matrix may still project onto that wrong space.

### Construct and verify a closest point

7. **Use both parts of the certificate.** A candidate $p$ must belong to the desired subspace and $b-p$ must be perpendicular to every generating direction. Neither membership alone nor perpendicularity to $p$ alone is sufficient.

8. **Prove uniqueness with the squared-distance split.** For every feasible $s$, ${\Vert b-s\Vert}^2={\Vert b-p\Vert}^2+{\Vert p-s\Vert}^2$. The final term is strictly positive when $s\ne p$, which proves more than stationarity.

9. **Use the line formula only with a nonzero generator.** For a line generated by $u$, $P=uu^T/(u^Tu)$. A zero generator describes the zero subspace, whose projector is zero; division by its squared length is undefined.

10. **Keep normalization with orthogonal generators.** The coefficient of a nonunit orthogonal generator is $(u_i^Tb)/(u_i^Tu_i)$. The denominators disappear only for an orthonormal list. Omitting them changes the operator's eigenvalues.

11. **Justify Gram invertibility.** For independent columns, $z^TC^TCz={\Vert Cz\Vert}^2>0$ for nonzero $z$. The proof fails when columns are dependent, regardless of whether the matrix dimensions happen to be square.

12. **Check basis invariance.** Replacing a basis $C$ by $CR$ with invertible $R$ must leave $P$ unchanged. Coefficients transform by $R^{-1}$. Physical projection is an operator on vectors, not a preferred coordinate convention.

13. **Use a normal when it is simpler.** A hyperplane through zero with normal $n$ has projector $I-nn^T/(n^Tn)$. The subtracted outer product projects onto the perpendicular normal line; it is not the hyperplane projector itself.

14. **Treat the sign of the residual consistently.** This chapter uses $r=b-p$. Some courses use $p-b$. Their orthogonality and squared-distance statements agree, but a displayed residual vector must have the chosen sign.

15. **Do not infer membership from a low residual alone.** Exact mathematics requires the stated equation. In approximate numerical work a tolerance and a norm must be declared. Rounding an entry to zero can change a rank or a feasibility decision.

### Recognize the operator algebraically

16. **Require symmetry and idempotence in ordinary real coordinates.** Together they characterize Euclidean orthogonal projection. Symmetry alone allows scaling, while idempotence alone allows oblique range-kernel decompositions.

17. **Interpret an idempotent without extra geometry.** Every idempotent fixes its range, kills its kernel and is diagonalizable into zero and one blocks. Its trace equals its rank even if its range and kernel are not perpendicular.

18. **Do not confuse orthogonal projections with orthogonal matrices.** A proper projection loses information and is singular. An orthogonal matrix preserves all norms and is invertible. Among orthogonal projectors only the identity is an orthogonal matrix on the whole ambient space.

19. **Use contraction with its universal quantifier.** An idempotent contracting every vector is orthogonal. Observing no expansion for one vector is inadequate; an oblique map can fix one direction while expanding another.

20. **Handle zero and full ranks explicitly.** The zero projector has operator norm zero; every nonzero orthogonal projector has operator norm one. Full rank forces an idempotent to be the identity. The determinant of a proper projector is zero.

21. **Reduce positive powers before multiplying large matrices.** Idempotence gives $P^j=P$ for positive integers. Therefore any polynomial in $P$ is determined by its values at zero and one. This works for oblique idempotents as well.

22. **Check which eigenspaces actually exist before imposing denominators.** The inverse formula for $aI+bP$ needs $a\ne0$ on a nonzero kernel and $a+b\ne0$ on a nonzero range. Endpoint ranks may remove one of those requirements.

23. **Use positivity only for the orthogonal case.** An orthogonal projector satisfies $x^TPx={\Vert Px\Vert}^2$ and $0\le x^TPx\le x^Tx$. A general oblique idempotent can fail these equalities and inequalities.

24. **Use the Frobenius norm with the correct object.** An orthogonal projector has squared Frobenius norm equal to its rank. This is a sum over all matrix entries, whereas the operator norm is a maximum over unit input vectors.

### Read coordinate and metric information

25. **Interpret a diagonal entry as retained coordinate energy.** For an orthogonal projector, $p_{ii}={\Vert Pe_i\Vert}^2$. Thus it lies between zero and one; the sum of all such entries is the integer rank.

26. **Exploit endpoint diagonal values.** A zero diagonal entry forces the corresponding row and column to vanish. A diagonal entry one forces that coordinate direction to be fixed with every other entry in its row and column zero.

27. **Use both off-diagonal bounds.** For distinct indices, $p_{ij}^2\le p_{ii}p_{jj}$ and $p_{ij}^2\le(1-p_{ii})(1-p_{jj})$. These come from the projector and its complement. They are necessary tests, not a complete existence theorem.

28. **Do not replace a matrix identity by its diagonal.** Bounds and integer trace can reject impossible candidates, but a matrix passing them may still fail idempotence. Off-diagonal equations can impose indispensable sign or magnitude conditions.

29. **Transform the metric under nonorthonormal coordinates.** If $x=Bz$, the coordinate metric is $B^TB$. The coordinate projector is $B^{-1}PB$ and satisfies metric self-adjointness even when ordinary symmetry is absent.

30. **Use the correct weighted normal direction.** For constraint row $D$ in metric $G$, the correction is in the span of $G^{-1}D^T$. Using $D^T$ instead generally solves the Euclidean problem rather than the weighted one.

31. **Keep positive definiteness as a real premise.** A positive-semidefinite form can assign zero cost to a nonzero direction, so closest points may not be unique. A singular metric cannot be substituted into an inverse formula derived for a norm without a separate argument.

32. **Preserve every complex conjugation.** For complex vectors, use $u^*u$, not $u^Tu$. The latter can vanish for a nonzero vector such as $(1,i)^T$. A convention difference in which argument is linear must be translated consistently.

### Combine subspaces only with justified hypotheses

33. **Check commutation before calling a product a projection.** For orthogonal projectors, $PQ$ is an orthogonal projector exactly when $PQ=QP$. In that case its range is the intersection. Without commutation, neither symmetry nor idempotence is automatic.

34. **Use sums for perpendicular ranges.** The plain sum $P+Q$ is a projector exactly when $PQ=0$ for orthogonal projectors. Overlapping directions would otherwise be counted twice. Perpendicular ranges are stronger than merely having zero intersection.

35. **Use the corrected sum for commuting overlap.** When the projectors commute, $P+Q-PQ$ projects onto the sum of the ranges. The subtraction removes their intersection. Without commutation, the expression can be nonsymmetric and wrong.

36. **Respect the sign of a contained-space difference.** If the range of $P$ lies inside the range of $Q$, then $Q-P$ projects onto the part added by $Q$. The reverse difference acts by minus one there and is generally not a projection.

37. **Treat product trace as overlap, not an integer dimension.** The value $\operatorname{tr}(PQ)={\Vert PQ\Vert}_F^2$ is nonnegative and may be fractional. For line projectors it is the squared cosine of the angle. It equals an intersection dimension in the commuting case.

38. **Use trace bounds with ambient dimension.** For ranks $r,s$ in dimension $m$, $\max(0,r+s-m)\le\operatorname{tr}(PQ)\le\min(r,s)$. The lower bound uses the unavoidable intersection and nonnegative contributions from other directions.

39. **Use saturation to prove containment.** If $\operatorname{tr}(PQ)=\operatorname{rank}(P)$, every unit basis vector of the range of $P$ must retain all its energy under $Q$. Thus that range is contained in the range of $Q$. The argument requires orthogonal positivity.

40. **Compare projectors through a matrix distance.** The identity ${\Vert P-Q\Vert}_F^2=r+s-2\operatorname{tr}(PQ)$ compares subspaces, not signed vector representatives. Changing a line's generator to its negative does not change its projector or this distance.

### Distinguish finite algorithms from exact operators

41. **Name the reflection convention.** This chapter uses $H=2P-I$, which fixes the range and negates its perpendicular complement. The alternative $I-2P$ reflects across the complementary space. Both square to the identity but fix different directions.

42. **Use symmetry when recovering a projector from a reflection.** If $H$ is symmetric and orthogonal, $(I+H)/2$ is an orthogonal projector. An arbitrary orthogonal rotation does not satisfy this conclusion because its square need not be the identity.

43. **Count alternating-projection cycles consistently.** A full cycle $PQ$ applies $Q$ first and then $P$. A display counting each application separately uses two steps per cycle. Reversing this convention changes the reported finite trajectory.

44. **Do not label a finite iterate as its limit.** Distinct nonperpendicular lines have zero intersection, but repeated projection can remain nonzero after every finite cycle. Its limit is zero. A simulation should show the finite vector and its convergence factor explicitly.

45. **Handle limiting angles before using logarithms.** The cycle count for two distinct acute lines uses a logarithm of a cosine strictly between zero and one. Perpendicular lines finish in one cycle, while coincident lines have a different intersection and no such decay formula.

46. **Keep rank-change examples separate from fixed-rank stability.** Generator matrices can converge while their column spaces drop rank at the limit. The limiting projector need not equal the projector of the limiting matrix. The example does not refute continuity on a fixed-rank neighborhood.

47. **Prefer applying an orthonormal basis over building a dense matrix.** Compute $Q(Q^Tb)$ directly when the basis is available. This retains exact geometric meaning and uses storage proportional to the basis size rather than the ambient matrix size.

48. **Do not equate mathematical formulas with stable code.** Normal equations square the full-column-rank spectral condition number. QR can avoid that additional squaring in the algorithm, but cannot remove intrinsic sensitivity or infer an undeclared numerical rank.

49. **Separate fitted-vector uniqueness from coefficient uniqueness.** A deficient generator matrix still has one physical closest point. Its coefficient minimizers differ by nullspace vectors. Only the minimum-norm one is perpendicular to the coefficient nullspace.

50. **Finish with an independent check.** Verify feasibility, residual perpendicularity, dimensions and an energy or direct-distance calculation. Agreement of these different certificates catches sign, normalization and space-selection errors that repeated multiplication of the same mistaken formula may miss.
