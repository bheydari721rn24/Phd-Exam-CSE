@@ 1|Unequal elementary masses and a many-to-one map|Original fiber-aggregation problem|map-weighted
An experiment has outcomes $a,b,c,d$ with probabilities $(1,2,3,4)/10$. Define $X(a)=X(c)=-1$, $X(b)=2$, $X(d)=5$. Find the PMF, $P(X>0)$, and $P(X=-1\mid X\le2)$. Explain why counting outcomes uniformly fails.
---SOLUTION---
The fiber for $-1$ contains $a,c$, so its mass is $(1+3)/10=2/5$. The other atoms have masses $1/5$ and $2/5$. These three masses are nonnegative and sum to one. The positive-value event contains $b,d$, with mass $(2+4)/10=3/5$. The conditioning event contains $a,b,c$, with mass $6/10$. Thus the requested conditional mass is $(4/10)/(6/10)=2/3$. Counting $a,c$ as two of four would give $1/2$, but the elementary outcomes were never equally likely. Aggregation adds supplied probabilities, not labels.

@@ 2|A normalized linear PMF and a conditional odd count|Original normalization problem
Let $p(k)=c(k+1)$ for $k=0,1,2,3,4$, zero elsewhere. Find $c$, $P(X\ge2)$ and $P(X\text{ is odd}\mid X\ge1)$.
---SOLUTION---
Normalization requires $c(1+2+3+4+5)=15c=1$, hence $c=1/15$. The upper event has numerator $3+4+5=12$, giving $4/5$. Given $X\ge1$, the retained numerator is $2+3+4+5=14$. Odd atoms are one and three, with numerator $2+4=6$, so the conditional answer is $6/14=3/7$. Using $2/5$ for the odd probability would assume uniform values. Dividing by the unconditional total 15 would answer the unconditioned odd event instead. The normalizing constant cancels in the conditional ratio because numerator and denominator use the same law.

@@ 3|Normalization leaves a parameter interval|Original parameter-classification problem
At atoms $-1,0,2$, masses are $(\theta,1-3\theta,2\theta)$. Classify all valid laws, all laws with three positive masses, and the values of $\theta$ for which $P(X\ge0)\ge3/4$.
---SOLUTION---
The masses always sum to one. Nonnegativity requires $\theta\ge0$ and $1-3\theta\ge0$, so valid laws have $0\le\theta\le1/3$. Three positive masses require $0<\theta<1/3$. The event $X\ge0$ excludes only $-1$ and has probability $1-\theta$. Its required bound gives $\theta\le1/4$. Intersecting this with the valid-law interval yields $[0,1/4]$. Solving the normalization equation alone would admit negative probabilities; treating the advertised atom list as requiring strict positivity would incorrectly delete legitimate degenerate laws unless that requirement is explicitly imposed.

@@ 4|A countable telescoping law|Original infinite-support problem
For $k\ge1$, let $p(k)=c/[k(k+1)]$. Find $c$, the exact CDF at any real $t$, and $P(X\ge10\mid X\ge4)$.
---SOLUTION---
Use $1/[k(k+1)]=1/k-1/(k+1)$. The sum through $m$ is $1-1/(m+1)$, tending to one, so $c=1$. For $t<1$, the CDF is zero; for $t\ge1$, it is $1-1/(\lfloor t\rfloor+1)$. The tail $P(X\ge r)=1/r$ for positive integer $r$. Since $\{X\ge10\}\subseteq\{X\ge4\}$, the conditional ratio is $(1/10)/(1/4)=2/5$. A finite partial sum below one is not a normalization defect: it has an explicit omitted tail. The conditional tail is not geometric, as its ratio depends on the starting threshold.

@@ 5|Two equations plus positivity|Original unknown-PMF problem
For values $0,1,2,3$, masses are $a,b,2b,a$. Given $P(X\le1)=2/5$, determine the full law and the most likely atom.
---SOLUTION---
The supplied CDF value gives $a+b=2/5$. Normalization gives $2a+3b=1$. Subtract twice the first equation to obtain $b=1/5$, hence $a=1/5$. The mass vector is $(1,1,2,1)/5$, so the unique modal atom is two. All masses are positive and the sum is one, confirming validity. Symmetry of the first and last entries does not make the middle masses equal. A mode concerns individual masses, not the cumulative probability; the largest CDF value at three does not make three the mode.

@@ 6|Invalid proposed CDF versus invalid PMF|Original conceptual multiple-choice problem
Which statement is necessarily correct? A: Every nonnegative function on a finite set is a PMF. B: A nondecreasing function with limits zero and one is automatically a CDF. C: A countable nonnegative mass table summing to one defines a discrete law. D: A PMF must be zero at noninteger inputs.
---SOLUTION---
Only C is correct: use the countable value set as the sample space and define event probabilities by summing the weights. A omits normalization; weights $(1,1)$ sum to two. B omits right continuity: the function zero for $t\le0$ and one for $t>0$ has the required limits and monotonicity but is not right-continuous at zero. D confuses discrete with integer-valued: a point mass at $\pi$ is discrete. The decisive conditions are nonnegative normalized masses and a countable concentrated set, not integrality or a particular graph shape.

@@ 7|A discrete law with an accumulation point|Original countable-support problem
Let $P(X=1/k)=2^{-k}$ for $k\ge1$. Find $P(X=0)$, $P(X\le1/3)$ and $F(0)$. Is zero in the closure of the atom set?
---SOLUTION---
The geometric-series sum of all masses is one, so no unassigned mass remains at zero: $P(X=0)=0$ and $F(0)=0$. The inequality $1/k\le1/3$ means $k\ge3$. Its tail sum is $\sum_{k=3}^{\infty}2^{-k}=1/4$. The atom values approach zero, so zero belongs to their topological closure even though it is not a positive-mass atom. This distinguishes the atom set from a closed-support convention. Calling the law continuous because its values approach zero would be wrong: all mass is concentrated on countably many atoms.

@@ 8|Repeated values in an empirical PMF|Original descriptive-law bridge
A stored list is $(-1,-1,0,2,2,2,5,5)$. A record is selected uniformly. Find the law of its value and compare it with selecting uniformly from the four distinct values. Find $P(X\ge2)$ in each experiment.
---SOLUTION---
Uniform record selection gives masses $(2,1,3,2)/8$ at $(-1,0,2,5)$. Its upper-event mass is $(3+2)/8=5/8$. Uniform distinct-value selection instead gives mass $1/4$ at each atom, so its upper-event mass is $1/2$. The two experiments have the same value set but different elementary sampling rules. Multiplicities must be retained when the list's records are the equally likely outcomes. This is an empirical law for the stated finite sampling experiment; it does not establish the unknown population distribution that may have generated the stored data.

@@ 9|A normalizer that cannot fix negative weights|Original validity classification
A proposed law has unnormalized weights $1,\alpha,1-\alpha,2$ on four distinct atoms. Determine which real $\alpha$ values permit normalization by one positive constant. Does normalizing the sum make $\alpha=2$ valid?
---SOLUTION---
The total raw weight is four, independent of $\alpha$, so the only normalizer is $1/4$. Positivity requires both $\alpha\ge0$ and $1-\alpha\ge0$, giving $0\le\alpha\le1$. At $\alpha=2$, the third raw weight is negative and remains negative after multiplication by $1/4$; normalization cannot repair it. If all four atoms must be positive, use the open interval $(0,1)$. A signed normalized table is not a probability law. Checking total mass and checking each sign are distinct necessary steps.

@@ 10|An interval-uniform law with congruence constraints|Original exact integer-count problem
$X$ is uniform on the integers from $-4$ through $8$. Find $P(0\le X<7,\ X\equiv1\pmod3)$ and $P(X\ge0\mid X\text{ is even})$.
---SOLUTION---
There are $8-(-4)+1=13$ atoms. The first event has feasible values one and four, so its probability is $2/13$. Even atoms are $-4,-2,0,2,4,6,8$, seven in total. Five of them are nonnegative, giving conditional probability $5/7$. Seven is excluded from the first event despite its congruence, because the inequality is strict. Zero is included in the second event and is even. Length of a real interval is not its integer atom count; counting the endpoints explicitly avoids both errors.

@@ 11|Four different intervals at two atoms|Original endpoint problem|cdf-three
For masses $(1/5,1/2,3/10)$ at $(-2,1,4)$, calculate $P(1<X\le4)$, $P(1\le X\le4)$, $P(1<X<4)$ and $P(1\le X<4)$ from CDF values.
---SOLUTION---
The relevant values are $F(1^-)=1/5$, $F(1)=7/10$, $F(4^-)=7/10$, $F(4)=1$. The four probabilities in the stated order are $1-7/10=3/10$, $1-1/5=4/5$, $7/10-7/10=0$, and $7/10-1/5=1/2$. Each formula removes precisely the lower excluded event from the upper included event. Reusing $F(b)-F(a)$ for all four would discard an included lower atom or add an excluded upper atom. The zero third answer is plausible: there is no support value strictly between one and four.

@@ 12|Recover a PMF from a CDF with noninteger atoms|Original CDF inversion problem
A right-continuous CDF equals zero below $-1$, $1/4$ on $[-1,1/2)$, $3/5$ on $[1/2,3)$ and one on $[3,\infty)$. Find its PMF and $P(-1<X<3)$.
---SOLUTION---
The jumps are $1/4$ at $-1$, $3/5-1/4=7/20$ at $1/2$, and $1-3/5=2/5$ at three. They sum to one. The strict interval contains only the middle atom, so its probability is $7/20$. The same result follows from $F(3^-)-F(-1)=3/5-1/4$. Using $F(3)-F(-1)$ would include the upper mass at three. Using an integer-step difference to recover the middle mass would be unjustified: the support contains a half-integer, so the left limit is the general tool.

@@ 13|A jump assigned to the wrong side|Original CDF correction problem
Someone proposes $H(t)=0$ for $t\le0$, $H(t)=2/5$ for $0<t\le2$, and $H(t)=1$ for $t>2$. Explain the exact defect and write the corrected CDF for masses $2/5$ at zero and $3/5$ at two.
---SOLUTION---
At zero the proposed function has value zero but right limit $2/5$; at two it has value $2/5$ but right limit one. It violates right continuity at both atoms. For the intended law, use zero on $t<0$, $2/5$ on $0\le t<2$, and one on $t\ge2$. The value at each atom must include the mass at that atom because a CDF uses $X\le t$. Correcting only a drawn dot while leaving the piecewise inequalities unchanged would retain the defect. Monotonicity and endpoint limits alone do not certify the original proposal.

@@ 14|Real thresholds for an integer-valued count|Original floor/ceiling problem
Let $P(X=k)=(k+1)/15$ for $k=0,\ldots,4$. Calculate $P(0.2\le X\le3.9)$ and $P(1<X<4)$, explaining every integer boundary.
---SOLUTION---
The first event contains integers one, two and three because its lower ceiling is one and upper floor is three. Its probability is $(2+3+4)/15=3/5$, or $F(3)-F(0)$. The second event contains only two and three, giving $(3+4)/15=7/15$, or $F(3)-F(1)$. The first includes one; the second excludes it. Rounding both real thresholds to the nearest integer would accidentally admit zero or four. CDF evaluation at a noninteger threshold includes only actual support values at or below it; it does not interpolate mass between integer atoms.

@@ 15|Mass at a threshold from two tail statements|Original reverse reconstruction problem
Suppose $P(X<2)=1/3$ and $P(X>2)=1/4$. Find $P(X=2)$, $F(2)$ and $P(X\ge2)$. Must $X$ be integer-valued?
---SOLUTION---
The three disjoint events below, at and above two partition all outcomes. Therefore the missing mass is $1-1/3-1/4=5/12$. Including this atom gives $F(2)=1/3+5/12=3/4$, while $P(X\ge2)=1-1/3=2/3$. Nothing about these event probabilities forces integer support elsewhere; remaining masses could be at noninteger values. The relation uses partition logic for any real variable. Replacing $P(X\ge2)$ by $1-F(2)$ would yield $1/4$, answering the strictly greater event instead.

@@ 16|CDF inequalities without an interpolation assumption|Original bounds problem
For an integer-valued $X$, $F(1)=1/5$ and $F(4)=4/5$. Find $P(1<X\le4)$. Determine the sharp possible range of $P(X=3)$ and construct endpoint examples.
---SOLUTION---
The interval probability is $4/5-1/5=3/5$. This mass is spread across atoms two, three and four, so the possible mass at three lies in $[0,3/5]$. For the lower endpoint, put masses $1/5,3/5,1/5$ at one, two, five. For the upper endpoint, put the same masses at one, three, five. Both constructions give the stated CDF values. Assigning $1/5$ to each of two, three, four would impose an unstated uniformity condition. A total interval mass does not determine its internal atom allocation.

@@ 17|Lower quantile at a cumulative boundary|Original quantile problem|quantile-three
For atoms $(-2,1,4)$ with masses $(1/5,1/2,3/10)$, find lower quantiles $Q(0.2),Q(0.21),Q(0.7),Q(0.71)$ and explain why $F(Q(u))$ can exceed $u$.
---SOLUTION---
The cumulative levels are $.2,.7,1$. The first value reaching the supplied level gives respectively $-2,1,1,4$. A jump crosses an interval of probability levels, so for $u=.21$ the selected atom one has $F(1)=.7>u$. Equality is not a requirement for a generalized inverse. At an exact cumulative boundary the lower quantile takes the earlier atom because its definition uses $F(x)\ge u$. The strict-exceedance digital sampling convention can choose the next atom at such a boundary; its law agrees under a continuously uniform input, whose probability of hitting that boundary is zero.

@@ 18|Digital inverse selection at exact endpoints|Original algorithm-contract problem|inverse-three
A sampler uses atoms $(-2,1,4)$ with cumulative probabilities $.2,.7,1$ and half-open input intervals on $[0,1)$. What outputs correspond to $U=0,.2,.699,.7,.999$? Which comparison implements this contract?
---SOLUTION---
The intervals are $[0,.2),[.2,.7),[.7,1)$, giving outputs $-2,1,1,4,4$. Scan cumulative values and select the first one strictly greater than $U$. Selecting the first cumulative value greater than or equal to $U$ would send $.2$ to $-2$ and $.7$ to one, violating the stated half-open convention. An ideal uniform real hits these exact boundaries with probability zero, but an implementation's test inputs can hit them exactly. Input one is outside the stated domain, so a robust implementation rejects it rather than inventing a last-atom convention.

@@ 19|A finite CDF with a remaining tail|Original conceptual decision problem
A plotted function jumps to $.2$ at zero, to $.5$ at one and to $.8$ at three, and remains $.8$ forever. It is nondecreasing and right-continuous. Can it be the full CDF of a finite real variable? Give two distinct valid repairs.
---SOLUTION---
No: a real variable's CDF tends to one at positive infinity. One repair adds an atom of mass $.2$ at a later finite value, such as five, so the final jump reaches one. Another rescales the three displayed masses by $1/.8$, yielding masses $1/4,3/8,3/8$ at zero, one, three; this changes the whole law and is justified only if that is the intended normalized truncated experiment. Treating the remaining $.2$ as mass at $+\infty$ would define an extended-valued variable, not the finite real variable asked for. The figure must disclose whether it is a prefix or a complete law.

@@ 20|Conditional interval from a CDF table|Original conditional-CDF problem
An integer-valued $X$ on $\{0,1,2,3,4\}$ has cumulative values $(.1,.3,.6,.8,1)$. Find $P(1<X\le3\mid X\ge1)$ and $P(X=2\mid X<4)$.
---SOLUTION---
The masses are successive differences $(.1,.2,.3,.2,.2)$. The first numerator is the mass at two and three,$.5$; the conditioning denominator is $1-.1=.9$, giving $5/9$. The second denominator is $F(3)=.8$, not $F(4)=1$, and its numerator is $.3$, giving $3/8$. Each desired event is already contained in its conditioning event, so the numerator needs no extra restriction. Subtracting $F(1^-)$ instead of $F(1)$ in the first numerator would wrongly include atom one. Using a CDF entry as an individual atom mass is another distinct error.

@@ 21|Squaring merges positive and negative preimages|Original transformation problem|square-five
Let atoms $-2,-1,0,1,2$ have masses $(1,2,4,5,8)/20$. Find the law of $Y=X^2$, then $P(Y\ge1)$ and $P(X>0\mid Y=1)$.
---SOLUTION---
Destination zero receives $4/20=1/5$; destination one receives $(2+5)/20=7/20$; destination four receives $(1+8)/20=9/20$. The upper-event probability is $(7+9)/20=4/5$. Given $Y=1$, the preimages are $X=-1$ and $X=1$; the positive one contributes $5/20$ of the retained $7/20$, giving $5/7$. Equal-magnitude values need not have equal probabilities. Counting both algebraic signs at zero would double its mass. Selecting only the positive square root would discard one valid preimage and fail normalization.

@@ 22|A decreasing affine map and an included endpoint|Original affine-CDF problem|affine-three
$X$ has masses $(1/5,1/2,3/10)$ at $(-2,1,4)$. Let $Y=3-2X$. Find $F_Y(1)$ and $P(Y<1)$ using $X$-events.
---SOLUTION---
Solve $3-2X\le1$ by subtracting three and dividing by negative two: this gives $X\ge1$. Its probability is $1-P(X<1)=1-1/5=4/5$. For $Y<1$, the reversed event is $X>1$, whose probability is $3/10$. The transformed atoms are seven, one,$-5$ with the original masses; their ordering reverses. The atom at $X=1$ maps exactly to $Y=1$ and belongs only to the nonstrict event. The formula $1-F_X(1)$ gives $3/10$ and therefore cannot answer $F_Y(1)$.

@@ 23|Modulo relabeling of a uniform finite interval|Original fiber-count problem
$X$ is uniform on $\{0,1,\ldots,10\}$ and $Y=X\bmod4$. Find its PMF and decide whether $Y$ is uniform. What changes if the upper endpoint is 11?
---SOLUTION---
The fibers for residues zero, one, two, three have sizes three, three, three, two. Their masses are $3/11,3/11,3/11,2/11$, so the first law is not uniform. Extending to $0,\ldots,11$ creates 12 equally likely elementary values, with three in every residue fiber, giving $1/4$ at each residue. @@ 24|Same marginals, three different equality probabilities|Reconstructed Harvard SP 4 Section 1 exercise family
Construct pairs $(X,Y)$ of fair Bernoulli variables with $P(X=Y)$ equal to zero, one, and $1/2$. Then identify the law of $X+Y$ in all three constructions.
---SOLUTION---
For zero equality, take $X=B,Y=1-B$ with $B$ fair; their sum is always one. For equality one, take $X=Y=B$; the sum has masses $1/2$ at zero and two. For equality $1/2$, generate two independent fair bits; the sum has masses $(1,2,1)/4$ at zero, one, two. All three pairs have identical one-variable marginals. Only the third permits multiplying the two atom probabilities. Thus equality of distributions neither establishes equality of variables nor determines their sum. The lesson extends Harvard's same-law contrast into a complete calculation with three joint constructions.

@@ 25|A cyclic shift preserves law but reverses one comparison|Reconstructed Harvard SP 4 Section 1 cyclic-label exercise family|cyclic-five
$X$ is uniform on $\{0,1,2,3,4\}$ and $Y=(X+2)\bmod5$. Find $P(X<Y)$,$P(X=Y)$ and both marginal laws.
---SOLUTION---
The deterministic pairs are $(0,2),(1,3),(2,4),(3,0),(4,1)$, each with probability $1/5$. Three satisfy $X<Y$, giving $3/5$, and none has equality. The map is a permutation of the five labels, so $Y$ is uniform just as $X$ is. This demonstrates equal laws without equality or independence. Multiplying $1/5$ by $1/5$ for an arbitrary pair would create 25 pairs, most of which are impossible in the actual experiment. The outcome table, not the two marginal PMFs, determines the comparison probability.

@@ 26|A threshold indicator of a nonuniform variable|Original Bernoulli construction|indicator-five
Let $P(X=k)=(k+1)/15$ for $k=0,\ldots,4$. Define $I=1_{\{X\ge3\}}$ and $J=1-I$. Find their laws and $P(I=1,J=1)$. Are $I,J$ independent?
---SOLUTION---
The threshold event has probability $(4+5)/15=3/5$, so $I$ is Bernoulli with parameter $3/5$. Its complement $J$ is Bernoulli with parameter $2/5$. They cannot both equal one, so their joint success probability is zero. The product of their marginal success probabilities is $6/25>0$, proving dependence. Each individual variable is binary, but that does not make different indicators independent. Their sum is always one; a sum of independent Bernoulli variables with those parameters would sometimes be zero or two.

@@ 27|Independent sum versus identical copy|Original convolution-dependence contrast
$X$ is uniform on $\{1,2,3\}$. Calculate $P(X+Y=4)$ when $Y$ is an independent copy and when $Y=X$. Explain why both scenarios have the same marginal law for $Y$.
---SOLUTION---
Independence gives nine equally likely ordered pairs. Three sum to four:$(1,3),(2,2),(3,1)$, so the probability is $1/3$. For $Y=X$, the sum equals $2X$ and is four only at $X=2$, also probability $1/3$. The requested probability happens to coincide, but the complete laws do not: the independent sum can be three or five, whereas $2X$ cannot. For example $P(X+Y=3)=2/9$ independently and zero for the identical copy. Agreement on one event does not prove independence or equality of distributions. Copying the same realization preserves the marginal but changes the joint experiment.

@@ 28|Maximum of two uniform dice from nested events|Reconstructed MIT Class 4 maximum exercise family|max-four
Two independent fair four-sided dice have values one through four. Derive the PMF of their maximum $M$ and calculate $P(M\ge3\mid M\le3)$.
---SOLUTION---
For integer $k=1,\ldots,4$, both dice are at most $k$ in $k^2$ of 16 pairs, so $F_M(k)=k^2/16$. Differencing gives masses $(1,3,5,7)/16$. The intersection in the conditional numerator is $M=3$, mass $5/16$; the denominator is $F_M(3)=9/16$, giving $5/9$. Multiplying a count of faces by two would double-count pairs where both faces satisfy the bound. @@ 29|Minimum and maximum strict tails|Original extrema problem
Three iid variables are uniform on $\{1,2,3,4\}$. Find $P(\min X_j>2)$ and $P(\max X_j=3)$. State where independence enters.
---SOLUTION---
Each variable exceeds two with probability $1/2$. Independence makes the conjunction probability $(1/2)^3=1/8$. The maximum equals three precisely when all values are at most three but not all at most two, giving $(3/4)^3-(2/4)^3=19/64$. Without independence, those products would need actual joint probabilities. Using $P(X_j\ge2)$ instead of $P(X_j>2)$ would include the atom at two and give the wrong first tail. The maximum mass is a difference of two nested cumulative events, not three times the individual mass at three.

@@ 30|Independent nonuniform convolution|Reconstructed MIT Class 4 weighted-sum exercise family|conv-weighted
$X$ has masses $1/3,2/3$ at zero, one. Independently $Y$ has masses $1/4,1/2,1/4$ at zero, one, two. Find the complete law of $Z=X+Y$ and $P(X=1\mid Z=2)$.

Normalization of this conditional pair law requires dividing by the entire diagonal mass, not by the marginal probability of $X=1$.

@@ 31|Exactly and at least in a reliability count|Original binomial calculation|bin-five-threequarter
Five independent devices work with probability $3/4$ each. Find the probabilities that exactly four work and at least four work. Explain why $\binom54(3/4)^4$ is not the first probability.
---SOLUTION---
The count is $\operatorname{Bin}(5,3/4)$. Exactly four requires one failed device, so its probability is $5(3/4)^4(1/4)=405/1024$. At least four adds the all-working case $(3/4)^5=243/1024$, giving $648/1024=81/128$. The omitted failure factor in the proposed expression changes an exact-count event into an overlapping collection where the fifth device may also work. The all-working outcome then belongs to each of five selected four-device witness sets. Exact-count sequences are disjoint; witness-selection events for an at-least property usually are not.

@@ 32|A short lower tail is the easiest route|Original binomial complement problem
Seven independent servers work with probability $4/5$ each. The network works if at least two work. Find its failure probability and state its success probability exactly.
---SOLUTION---
Failure means zero or one working server. The count is $\operatorname{Bin}(7,4/5)$. These disjoint masses are $(1/5)^7$ and $7(4/5)(1/5)^6$. Their sum is $(1+28)/5^7=29/78125$. Network success is its complement $78096/78125$. A long upper-tail sum gives the same result but takes more work. Treating network failure as one-server failure probability $1/5$ ignores redundancy. Omitting zero from “fewer than two” would drop a legitimate failure outcome. The independence and common working probability are explicit modeling assumptions.

@@ 33|A binomial parameter from adjacent masses|Original reverse parameter problem
$X\sim\operatorname{Bin}(5,p)$ with $0<p<1$, and $P(X=3)=2P(X=2)$. Find $p$ and $P(X=0)$.
---SOLUTION---
The ratio of adjacent masses at three and two is $((5-2)/(2+1))(p/(1-p))=p/(1-p)$. Setting this to two yields $p=2/3$. Then the zero-success mass is $(1-p)^5=(1/3)^5=1/243$. Using $P(3)/P(2)=p$ would omit the failure-factor ratio. Evaluating both factorial expressions first is valid but unnecessary; cancellation exposes the parameter directly. The assumptions exclude zero masses at endpoint parameters, so the ratio is well-defined.

@@ 34|One or two binomial modes|Original parameter classification
For $X\sim\operatorname{Bin}(9,p)$,$0<p<1$, determine when four is a mode, when it is the unique mode, and when three and four are tied modes.
---SOLUTION---
An atom four is modal when $(n+1)p=10p$ lies in $[4,5]$, giving $p\in[.4,.5]$. It is unique for $p\in(.4,.5)$, because then the noninteger modal index is $\lfloor10p\rfloor=4$. At $p=.4$, the integer value $10p=4$ creates tied modes three and four; at $p=.5$, the tied modes are four and five. Adjacent-ratio comparisons prove these classifications, not mere symmetry. Rounding $np$ to the nearest integer can fail at tie boundaries and does not provide the exact interval.

@@ 35|Binomial parity through a signed expansion|Original hard binomial identity|bin-four-quarter
For $X\sim\operatorname{Bin}(n,p)$ with $n\ge1$, derive $P(X\text{ is even})$. Evaluate it for $n=4,p=1/4$ and explain when it equals $1/2$.
---SOLUTION---
Let $E,O$ be the even and odd probability sums. Normalization gives $E+O=1$. The signed binomial expansion gives $E-O=((1-p)-p)^n=(1-2p)^n$. Adding the equations yields $E=[1+(1-2p)^n]/2$. At $n=4,p=1/4$, this is $(1+1/16)/2=17/32$. For finite $n\ge1$, equality to $1/2$ requires $p=1/2$. Uniformity of the binary sequence space holds only at that parameter. With $n=0$, the empty count is even surely, so the separate degenerate case gives one.

@@ 36|Independent trials with unequal probabilities|Original Poisson-binomial calculation|unequal-two
Two independent trials have success probabilities $1/4$ and $3/4$. Find the count law and compare $P(X=1)$ with the binomial law formed from their mean probability.

@@ 73|A conditional geometric family-size law|Original countable mixture bridge
Let $P(N=n)=(1-a)a^n$ for $n\ge0$, where $0<a<1$. Given $N$, each object's independent attribute occurs with probability $b$, where $0<b<1$. Condition on no object having the attribute. Derive the conditional law of $N$ and $P(N\ge1)$ under that condition.
---SOLUTION---
The no-attribute likelihood at size $n$ is $(1-b)^n$, including one at $n=0$. Joint masses are $(1-a)[a(1-b)]^n$. Their sum is $(1-a)/(1-a(1-b))$. Dividing gives the normalized geometric failure-count law with mass $[1-a(1-b)][a(1-b)]^n$. Its positive-size probability is $a(1-b)$. The likelihood changes the whole size law; using the original $P(N\ge1)=a$ would ignore selection. Excluding zero before normalization would answer a different conditioning event. All factors lie in the stated open intervals, so the series and denominator are well-defined.

@@ 74|Random eligibility with an unequal-trial count|Original integrative Poisson-binomial problem
Three independent objects qualify with probabilities $1/2,2/3,3/4$. Given qualification, each independently succeeds with probability $1/2$. Find the probability exactly two objects ultimately succeed.
---SOLUTION---
The ultimate per-object success probabilities are $1/4,1/3,3/8$. Exactly two consists of three disjoint patterns. Their probabilities are $(1/4)(1/3)(5/8)=5/96$,$(1/4)(2/3)(3/8)=6/96$, and $(3/4)(1/3)(3/8)=9/96$. Summing gives $20/96=5/24$. Independence of the complete per-object experiments supports these products, but their unequal probabilities prevent a simple binomial formula. Replacing eligibility by its average before applying thinning would alter the final count law. This combines event construction with a distinct-pattern sum.

@@ 75|A uniform variable's quadratic preimages|Original nonlinear support problem|quadratic-six
$X$ is uniform on $\{-2,-1,0,1,2,3\}$. Find the law of $Y=X(X-1)$ and $P(X\ge1\mid Y=2)$.
---SOLUTION---
Evaluate the map in the supplied order to obtain $6,2,0,0,2,6$. The destination atoms zero, two, six each have two preimages, so their masses are all $1/3$. Given $Y=2$, the preimages are $X=-1$ and $X=2$, each equally likely; only the latter satisfies $X\ge1$, giving $1/2$. Uniformity happens here because the fiber sizes are equal; it is not a general consequence of applying a polynomial to a uniform input. Solving the quadratic equation without checking the original support could introduce invalid roots in other examples.

@@ 76|Recovering a finite law from CDF and transformed events|Original hard reconstruction problem
$X$ has atoms $-2,-1,1,3$ with unknown masses $a,b,c,d$. Given $F(-1)=1/2$,$P(X^2=1)=3/5$ and $P(X\ge1)=1/2$, determine all possible laws. Is the information sufficient for a unique law?
---SOLUTION---
The equations are $a+b=1/2$,$b+c=3/5$,$c+d=1/2$. The last equation already follows from normalization and the first. Taking $b=t$ gives $a=1/2-t$,$c=3/5-t$,$d=t-1/10$. Nonnegativity requires $1/10\le t\le1/2$, so there is an interval of valid laws, not a unique one. All four atoms are positive only for the open interval. At the endpoints one atom vanishes. Counting supplied statements as automatically independent equations would wrongly promise unique reconstruction; event equations can be redundant.

@@ 77|Two independent binomials with common probability|Original convolution and conditional allocation
Independent $X\sim\operatorname{Bin}(2,p)$ and $Y\sim\operatorname{Bin}(3,p)$ have $0<p<1$. Derive the sum law and $P(X=1\mid X+Y=2)$.
---SOLUTION---
Concatenating the two independent trial blocks gives five iid Bernoulli trials, so $X+Y\sim\operatorname{Bin}(5,p)$. For the conditional allocation, the numerator is $\binom21\binom31p^2(1-p)^3=6p^2(1-p)^3$. The denominator is $\binom52p^2(1-p)^3=10p^2(1-p)^3$, giving $3/5$. Conditional on two successes among five exchangeable trial positions, the allocation is hypergeometric. The cancellation depends on the common $p$ and the nondegenerate parameter interval; unequal success probabilities would retain parameter factors.

@@ 78|A conditional maximum with ties|Original hard extrema calculation|max-four
Two independent fair four-sided dice are $A,B$. Given $\max(A,B)=3$, find $P(A=B)$ and $P(A=3)$.
---SOLUTION---
The maximum-three event contains five equally likely pairs:$(1,3),(2,3),(3,1),(3,2),(3,3)$. Exactly one has a tie, so the first conditional probability is $1/5$. Three have $A=3$, so the second is $3/5$. Counting $A=3$ and $B=3$ as disjoint categories would count the pair $(3,3)$ twice. Their union has $3+3-1=5$ eligible pairs. The sample-space denominator after conditioning is five, not the original 16. A maximum constraint induces conditional dependence even though the original dice were independent.

@@ 79|A normalized PMF can have no finite first moment|Original advanced boundary problem
For $P(X=k)=1/[k(k+1)]$,$k\ge1$, verify normalization and determine whether $\sum_{k\ge1}kp(k)$ is finite. Explain why this does not invalidate the law.
---SOLUTION---
Normalization telescopes to one as in Question 4. But $kp(k)=1/(k+1)$, whose sum diverges by comparison with the harmonic series. Since all terms are nonnegative, the extended first moment is $+\infty$. A proper countable PMF only requires its mass sum to converge to one; it does not require its value-weighted sum to be finite. This distinction introduces an essential condition for the next expectation chapter. Truncating the support at a displayed cutoff would produce a finite sum, but would change or incompletely represent the original law.

@@ 80|A complete model audit with a misleading average|Original integrative conceptual multiple-choice problem
Ten binary devices each work with marginal probability $.8$. Which conclusion follows without any further dependence assumption? A: Their working count is $\operatorname{Bin}(10,.8)$. B: The chance all ten work is $.8^{10}$. C: Each device's individual indicator is Bernoulli $.8$. D: Their count must attain every integer from zero through ten with positive probability. Construct a counterexample to every false conclusion.
---SOLUTION---
Only C follows: each binary indicator has success mass $.8$ and failure mass $.2$. For a single counterexample to A, B, D, let one Bernoulli $.8$ event decide whether all ten work together. The count then has masses $.2$ at zero and $.8$ at ten, and every intermediate count is impossible. All-work probability is $.8$, not $.8^{10}$. Each individual marginal remains correct. Mutual independence would justify A and B, and with $0<p<1$ also give full count support, but it is absent from the contract. A marginal probability cannot supply an unstated joint experiment.
