@@ 2|A swap ledger in a four-by-four determinant|Berkeley worked calculation, independently reconstructed|eliminate-main

The model retains the original matrix, current matrix, and correction relation through every checkpoint.

**Transfer rule.** A triangular diagonal product belongs to the transformed matrix; correct it for every determinant-changing operation.
@@ 3|Choosing a sparse expansion|Cambridge computation, independently reconstructed|cofactor-sparse

@@ 5|A four-by-four counterexample to Sarrus extension|Original conceptual counterexample

---SOLUTION---
Use the permutation $(2,1,3,4)$, whose matrix exchanges the first two rows of $I_4$. Its determinant is minus one because it is a single transposition. Its unique nonzero determinant product selects columns $(2,1,3,4)$. That selection is neither a cyclic shift of $(1,2,3,4)$ nor one of the reverse cyclic diagonal patterns, so a rule retaining only the four forward and four reverse wrapped diagonals misses it and returns zero. @@ 6|Scaling the whole matrix and its inverse|Original formula problem

@@ 8|Simultaneous row changes are not sequential replacements|Original conceptual problem
Rows $r_1,r_2,r_3$ of $A$ are replaced simultaneously by $r_1+r_2$, $r_1-r_2$, and $2r_3$. Express the new determinant in terms of $\det A$.
---SOLUTION---
The new matrix is $TA$, where $T=\begin{bmatrix}1&1&0\\1&-1&0\\0&0&2\end{bmatrix}$. Its determinant is $(-1-1)2=-4$, so the new determinant is $-4\det A$. The second new row uses the original first row, not the already changed first row. Treating the two transformations as sequential row replacements would therefore describe a different matrix. @@ 9|Affine row replacement|Original proof problem
With all other rows unchanged, replace row $i$ by $\alpha r_i+\beta r_j+\gamma r_k$, where $i,j,k$ are distinct. What happens to the determinant, including when $\alpha=0$?
---SOLUTION---
Multilinearity splits the new determinant into three terms. The first is $\alpha\det A$. The second has its new row $i$ equal to row $j$, so it vanishes; the third similarly repeats row $k$. Therefore the result is $\alpha\det A$ for all scalar coefficients. When $\alpha=0$, the new row belongs to the span of two retained rows and the determinant is zero. The transformation then need not be invertible. No assumptions about nonzero $\beta$ or $\gamma$ are needed, and no whole-matrix additivity law is being used.

**Transfer rule.** Row linearity is useful because retained duplicate rows eliminate the unwanted terms.
@@ 10|A diagonal product with a zero first pivot|Original calculation

---SOLUTION---

@@ 11|Anti-diagonal matrices|Original general formula
An order-$n$ matrix has only anti-diagonal entries $b_1,\ldots,b_n$, in row order. Derive its determinant and apply it to $(2,3,5,7)$.
---SOLUTION---
Only the reversing permutation survives. Every pair is an inversion, so there are $n(n-1)/2$ inversions. The determinant is $(-1)^{n(n-1)/2}\prod_i b_i$. This formula includes zero anti-diagonal entries: the product then vanishes. For order four the inversion count is six and the sign is positive, so the requested determinant is $2\cdot3\cdot5\cdot7=210$. A reversal is not generally a single swap; counting it as one would give the wrong sign in this case. One may also swap rows into diagonal order and track the same parity.

**Transfer rule.** Reversal signs depend on the number of inverted pairs, not on the visual direction of a diagonal.
@@ 12|A cycle and a permutation power|Original conceptual problem
Let $P$ permute six coordinates with disjoint cycles of lengths three, two, and one. Find $\det P$ and $\det(P^{2026})$.
---SOLUTION---
A cycle of length three has sign $(-1)^2=1$, a two-cycle has sign minus one, and a fixed point has sign one. Their product is minus one. Equivalently, six elements and three cycles give sign $(-1)^{6-3}=-1$. By multiplicativity, the determinant of the even power is $(-1)^{2026}=1$. This does not imply that the matrix power itself is identity: the permutation order is the least common multiple of the cycle lengths, namely six, and 2026 is not a multiple of six. Determinant discards that finer cycle information.

**Transfer rule.** The determinant of a permutation power reveals parity, not its full permutation order.
@@ 13|Whole-matrix additivity fails even for invertible matrices|Original counterexample

---SOLUTION---
Take $A=I_2$ and $B=-I_2$. Both are invertible and both determinants are one because the dimension is even. Their sum is the zero matrix and has determinant zero. @@ 16|An untransposed cofactor matrix|Original calculation|adjugate-main

---SOLUTION---
The signed cofactors give $C=\begin{bmatrix}4&-3\\-1&2\end{bmatrix}$. Transposing yields $\operatorname{adj}A=\begin{bmatrix}4&-1\\-3&2\end{bmatrix}$. Since the determinant is five, the inverse is one fifth of that adjugate. @@ 17|Wrong-row cofactor sums|Original conceptual identity
For a general three-by-three matrix, evaluate $\sum_{j=1}^3 a_{2j}C_{1j}$ and explain the result without assuming invertibility.
---SOLUTION---
Replace row one by row two while leaving the other rows unchanged. The cofactors along the replaced row remain those of the original first row because their minors exclude that entire row. Expanding the new determinant along row one gives the requested sum. The new matrix has two equal rows, so the determinant and sum are zero. This proof works for every original matrix, including singular ones. @@ 18|Rank and adjugate rank|Original classification problem
For real matrices of order four, determine the adjugate rank when the original rank is four, three, two, or zero.
---SOLUTION---
At rank four the matrix is invertible and its adjugate is a nonzero scalar times its inverse, so its rank is four. At rank three some three-by-three minor is nonzero, making the adjugate nonzero, while every adjugate column lies in the one-dimensional nullspace by $A\operatorname{adj}A=0$. Its rank is therefore one. At rank two or zero all three-by-three minors vanish, so the adjugate is zero and has rank zero. There is no case of adjugate rank two or three for order four. The argument uses maximal minors and nullspace dimension together.

**Transfer rule.** For order at least two, adjugate rank is full, one, or zero according to the original rank threshold.
@@ 19|The one-dimensional adjugate exception|Original boundary problem
Compute the adjugate of $[0]$ and of the zero two-by-two matrix. Explain the difference.
---SOLUTION---
Deleting the sole row and column of a one-by-one matrix leaves the empty matrix, whose determinant is defined as one. Its cofactor and adjugate are therefore $[1]$, even when the original entry is zero. For the zero two-by-two matrix, each minor is a one-by-one zero matrix, so every cofactor is zero and the adjugate is zero. The frequently used rank rule assumes order at least two. Applying its low-rank conclusion to order one without checking the convention produces a false answer and breaks the inverse/cofactor framework at its base case.

**Transfer rule.** Verify the matrix order before applying adjugate formulas involving zeroth powers or empty minors.

@@ 21|Cramer's rule with signed numerators|Original worked system|cramer-main

@@ 23|A parameter family with two different singular ranks|Oxford structured determinant, independently reconstructed|parameter-main

@@ 33|A tridiagonal determinant with zero intermediate pivots|MIT recurrence, independently reconstructed|recurrence-main
An order-$n$ tridiagonal matrix has ones on all three bands. Find its determinant when $n=2026$ and explain why pivot division is risky.
---SOLUTION---
The recurrence is $D_n=D_{n-1}-D_{n-2}$ with $D_0=D_1=1$. It gives $1,1,0,-1,-1,0$ and repeats after six steps because the ordered pair of consecutive values returns to its initial pair. @@ 35|A nonconstant tridiagonal recurrence|Original medium calculation

**Transfer rule.** In a variable-band recurrence, match each off-diagonal product to the edge just appended.
@@ 36|Signed area and unsigned triangle area|Original geometry problem|geometry-main
Let $u=(2,1)^T$, $v=(-1,3)^T$. Find the signed parallelogram area, physical parallelogram area, and the area of the triangle with vertices $0,u,v$.
---SOLUTION---
The determinant is $2\cdot3-(-1)\cdot1=7$, so the ordered columns have positive orientation. The physical parallelogram area is $|7|=7$, and the triangle occupies half of that parallelogram, giving $7/2$. Reversing the column order changes the signed determinant to minus seven but does not change either physical area. A triangle with nonzero base point must use edge differences from that base point, not the absolute vertex coordinates directly in a two-by-two determinant. The attached geometry uses these exact two vectors and labels the orientation separately from physical area.

**Transfer rule.** Keep determinant sign for orientation, but use its absolute value for geometric area.
@@ 37|A triangle translated away from the origin|Original geometry calculation
Compute the area of the triangle with vertices $(1,2)$, $(4,3)$, and $(2,6)$.
---SOLUTION---
Use the first vertex as the base point. The edge vectors are $(3,1)$ and $(1,4)$, so their determinant is $12-1=11$. The triangle area is $11/2$. Using the other vertex as base point gives different edge coordinates but the same absolute determinant, because translation does not change area. Equivalently, the determinant of the three-by-three matrix with rows $(x_i,y_i,1)$ is 11 in this order, and half its magnitude is the same answer. Multiplying the absolute coordinates of just two vertices would ignore the base-point translation.

**Transfer rule.** Convert vertices into edge differences or use homogeneous-coordinate triangle determinants.
@@ 38|Gram volume for a rectangular matrix|Original calculation|gram-main
Let $u=(1,0,1)^T$ and $v=(0,2,1)^T$. Find the area of their parallelogram using a Gram determinant.
---SOLUTION---
The Gram matrix is $\begin{bmatrix}u^Tu&u^Tv\\v^Tu&v^Tv\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&5\end{bmatrix}$. Its determinant is nine, so the area is three. Independently, the three row-subset minors of $[u\ v]$ are two, one, and minus two; their squared sum is $4+1+4=9$, illustrating Cauchy–Binet. The original three-by-two matrix has no ordinary determinant. The square root of its Gram determinant is an unsigned two-dimensional volume embedded in three-dimensional space, not an orientation sign for the surrounding space.

**Transfer rule.** For rectangular columns, compute volume through the square Gram matrix and take a square root.
@@ 39|Cauchy–Binet with cancellation|Original rectangular product problem
For $X=\begin{bmatrix}1&2&0\\0&1&3\end{bmatrix}$ and $Y=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&0\\0&1\end{bmatrix}$, compute $\det(XY)$ directly and by maximal minors.
---SOLUTION---
The product is $\begin{bmatrix}4&1\\1&3\end{bmatrix}$ and its determinant is 11. For column subsets $(1,2)$, $(1,3)$, and $(2,3)$, the minors of $X$ are one, three, and six. The corresponding row minors of $Y$ are minus one, two, and one. Their product sum is $-1+6+6=11$. The negative first contribution is essential: Cauchy–Binet uses signed products, not squared minors unless the second factor is the transpose or conjugate transpose of the first. Consistent ordering of each selected subset is also necessary.

**Transfer rule.** Match the same ordered intermediate index subset in both rectangular factors.
@@ 40|The impossible tall product inverse|Original rank problem
If $X$ is five-by-three and $Y$ is three-by-five, determine $\det(XY)$. Can $YX$ nevertheless be invertible?
---SOLUTION---
The image of $XY$ is contained in the image of $X$, whose dimension is at most three. Therefore the five-by-five product has rank at most three and determinant zero. The three-by-three reverse product can be invertible: choose $X$ whose first three rows form $I_3$ and whose last two rows are zero, and choose $Y$ to extract the first three coordinates. Then $YX=I_3$, while $XY$ is the projection onto the first three coordinates of five-dimensional space. Rectangular products do not share the square-factor determinant multiplication law.

**Transfer rule.** Compare the product's output dimension with the intermediate dimension before attempting determinant arithmetic.
