@@ 1|BFS queue and parent choices|Original exact trace|bfs-main
For $S\to A,B$, $A\to C,D$, $B\to D,G$, and $D\to G$, run discovery-on-insertion BFS, testing goals on removal. Every edge costs one. Give the selected order, expanded order and returned path.
---SOLUTION---
Start with $[S]$. After expanding $S,A,B,C,D$, the successive queues are $[A,B]$, $[B,C,D]$, $[C,D,G]$, $[D,G]$, and $[G]$. The duplicate $D$ from $B$ and duplicate $G$ from $D$ are rejected. Selection order is $S,A,B,C,D,G$; expansion order omits the goal $G$. Its immutable parent is $B$, so the path is $S,B,G$ with depth and cost two. Discovery fixes the first minimum-depth parent, not the most recently encountered predecessor.
---RULE---
In FIFO BFS with discovery on insertion, a state's first parent records a minimum-edge route. A selected goal is not expanded under this chapter's convention, so selection and expansion lists differ at termination.
@@ 2|Early BFS goal testing|Original counting comparison|bfs-main
For Question 1, move the goal test to generation, while checking the root separately. How many non-goal states are expanded, and does the returned path change?
---SOLUTION---
The root is not a goal. Expand $S$ and $A$ as before. During expansion of $B$, its first child $D$ is a known duplicate and its next child is $G$. Return immediately with the parent route $S,B,G$. Exactly three states, $S,A,B$, have been expanded. The path remains minimum depth because all previously generated nodes have depth at most two and no shallower goal was found. The operation saving does not authorize early goal testing in UCS with variable costs.
---RULE---
BFS can accept a newly generated goal while preserving minimum depth if the root is checked and FIFO layer order is maintained. Its expansion count then differs from a goal-on-removal implementation.
@@ 3|DFS stack direction|Original exact trace|dfs-main
Use Question 1's graph. DFS uses a stack with the top at the right, discovery on insertion, and the written successor order as the desired removal order. Trace the result.
---SOLUTION---
Push $B$ and then $A$, so $A$ is removed first. After expanding $A$, push $D$ then $C$ above the pending $B$. The stack is $[B,D,C]$. Removing leaf $C$ exposes $D$, which inserts $G$. The selected order is $S,A,C,D,G$ and the returned route is $S,A,D,G$, with three unit-cost edges. The known pending state $B$ prevents duplicate discovery if reached elsewhere. An implementation that pushes $A$ then $B$ instead explores the other branch first.
---RULE---
For a right-top stack, reverse the desired successor removal order when pushing. DFS's successor scheduling changes its first solution and parent tree; a trace must declare this convention.
@@ 4|A weighted BFS counterexample|Original optimization counterexample|weighted-bfs
Let $S\to G$ cost 9 and $S\to A\to G$ cost 1 on each edge. Which route does BFS return, and what is the optimal cost?
---SOLUTION---
BFS discovers the direct goal at depth one and the alternative goal node lies at depth two. It returns the direct edge costing nine, regardless of whether the depth-one $A$ is selected before $G$: discovery of $G$ already fixes its first parent. The alternative costs $1+1=2$. BFS is correct for minimum actions but not for minimum total cost. Positivity of all costs does not make action count equivalent to cost, because individual edge magnitudes differ.
---RULE---
Positive unequal costs do not make BFS cost-optimal. Always distinguish the minimum-depth objective from the minimum-sum-of-costs objective; the two can choose different routes even on three vertices.
@@ 5|Relaxation is essential in UCS|Original exact trace|ucs-main
Run UCS on $S\to A:4$, $S\to B:1$, $B\to A:1$, $A\to G:2$, $B\to G:8$. Use stable insertion ties and a lazy heap.
---SOLUTION---

The old $A:4$ has earlier insertion order than $G:4$, so it is popped first and discarded as stale. The valid goal then returns $S,B,A,G$ at cost four. @@ 6|The first generated UCS goal is expensive|Original stopping counterexample|early-goal
Let $S\to G:10$, $S\to A:1$, $A\to G:1$. Why does returning on goal generation fail?
---SOLUTION---
Expanding the start immediately generates a goal route costing ten, but also a route to $A$ costing one. The latter leads to a goal of cost two. Returning the generated goal ignores unresolved cheaper prefixes. Correct UCS first selects $A:1$, improves the goal to two, and accepts that valid minimum-cost goal on removal. The contradiction proof requires the goal to beat all valid frontier prefixes, a fact that generation alone cannot establish. All edges are positive, so negative costs are not the cause.
---RULE---

@@ 7|A Boolean discovered bit loses the cheapest route|Original duplicate counterexample|ucs-main

The start discovers $A$ at cost four and $B$ at cost one. After selecting $B$, the route to $A$ at cost two is rejected by the Boolean bit. A goal at cost nine is admitted. @@ 8|Equal-cost duplicate suppression|Original zero-cycle reasoning|zero-cycle
The graph has $S\to A:0$, $A\to S:0$, and $A\to G:2$. Explain why strict cost improvement is safe and why admitting every equal-cost duplicate is dangerous.
---SOLUTION---
The first discovered labels are zero for $S$ and $A$. The return edge proposes another cost-zero route to $S$, but strict improvement rejects it. The goal is eventually selected at cost two. If every equal-cost duplicate is inserted without a settled or path-cycle rule, the zero cycle creates unboundedly many cost-zero paths, all ahead of the cost-two goal. @@ 9|Negative suffix without a cycle|Original proof counterexample|negative-edge

@@ 10|Positive edges with no positive lower bound|Original infinite-space counterexample|starvation-main
The root has a goal edge of cost 1 and a chain whose successive edge costs are $2^{-1},2^{-2},\ldots$. Will UCS find the goal?
---SOLUTION---
After $k$ chain edges, the accumulated cost is $1-2^{-k}$, strictly below one for every finite $k$. Each selected chain node therefore inserts another prefix that outranks the waiting goal. There are no repeated chain states and every individual edge is strictly positive. UCS never removes the goal. This shows why positivity and a uniform positive lower bound are different assumptions. Strict positivity of individual edge costs does not ensure UCS completeness in an infinite graph. A positive uniform edge bound, or another finite-sublevel condition, is needed to exclude infinitely many cheaper prefixes.

@@ 12|The unary exception|Original limit case|counts-chain

---SOLUTION---

@@ 18|DLS goal at the boundary|Original boundary case|dls-main

---SOLUTION---
The goal lies exactly two edges from the root, so it is within the allowed depth. Correct DLS tests $G$ for success before deciding whether it may expand further and returns the two-action path. @@ 19|Cutoff is not failure|Original outcome semantics|dls-main
At limit 1, DLS reaches non-goal $A$ in $S\to A\to G$. Explain the required result and its consequence for IDS.
---SOLUTION---
The route to $G$ has not been searched because expansion from $A$ would exceed the current limit. The result is cutoff, not a proof of impossibility. IDS must increase the limit and retry. If a failed child branch is combined with a cutoff child branch, the parent result remains cutoff unless another branch supplies a solution. Returning failure after the first dead end would ignore unresolved branches. Genuine failure means all eligible branches are exhausted without a truncated possibility under the declared boundary convention.
---RULE---
DLS needs solution, cutoff and failure as distinct outcomes. Any unresolved cutoff propagates through an otherwise unsuccessful parent; IDS may stop only on a solution or genuine failure.
@@ 20|The depth-sensitive duplicate trap|Original counterexample|depth-alias-main

@@ 22|An acyclic infinite DFS trap|Original completeness counterexample|dfs-infinite

---SOLUTION---
No state repeats on the infinite first branch, so cycle checking rejects nothing. DFS always schedules the next chain node ahead of the root's second child and never reaches the goal. @@ 23|Finite graph DFS complexity|Original input-measure comparison
A reachable adjacency-list graph has 120 vertices and 430 directed edges. Explain the asymptotic BFS/DFS work with discovery on insertion and why $O(b^m)$ is not the best explicit-graph bound.
---SOLUTION---
Each vertex is accepted at most once. Its adjacency list is enumerated once when expanded, so at most 430 edge entries and 120 vertices are processed before exhaustion. Complexity depends on the input measure. Global-discovery BFS/DFS are linear in an explicit graph's vertices and edges, while an implicitly defined search space can still grow exponentially with depth.

If the goal wins the tie, only one entry is selected. If it loses every tie, all eight are selected, including seven leaf expansions. @@ 26|Stable heap serial numbers|Original implementation reasoning
Why store `(cost, serial, node)` rather than `(cost, node)` in a Python heap when state objects have no ordering?
---SOLUTION---
Heap tuple comparison first compares costs. On a tie, `(cost, node)` attempts to compare node records, potentially reaching unordered state objects and raising an exception. A strictly increasing integer serial resolves every equal-cost comparison before node data is inspected. It also defines a reproducible insertion-order tie policy for traces. The serial is bookkeeping, not an edge cost and not a heuristic estimate. It must not alter the numerical cost used for relaxation or optimality checks.
---RULE---
Use a separate stable tie key for heap records whose states are not orderable. Tie bookkeeping determines reproducibility but must not be mixed into the mathematical path cost.

@@ 29|Adding a constant to edge costs|Original objective transformation
Route A has one edge costing 7; route B has three edges costing 2 each. Add 2 to every edge. Does the preferred route change?
---SOLUTION---
Originally A costs seven and B costs six, so B is preferred. After the shift, A costs nine and B costs twelve, so A is preferred. The transformed path cost is $g+2d$, not $g+2$ for the whole route. @@ 30|Positive scaling preserves order|Original algebraic proof
Prove that multiplying every edge cost by $\alpha>0$ preserves the set of minimum-cost solutions. What happens at $\alpha=0$ or $\alpha<0$?
---SOLUTION---
Every complete path cost becomes $\alpha g$. For positive $\alpha$, $g_1<g_2$ holds exactly when $\alpha g_1<\alpha g_2$, and equalities are preserved. Thus all minimizers remain the same. At zero, all finite path costs become zero and previously inferior solutions tie. For a negative multiplier, strict inequalities reverse: former maximizers become minimizers when such extrema exist. Negative transformed edges can also invalidate UCS even when the algebraic ranking question is well defined.
---RULE---
Strictly positive cost scaling preserves comparisons and minimizers. Zero scaling destroys distinctions, and negative scaling reverses the objective and can violate the search algorithm's edge assumptions.

@@ 38|Collision-free joint states|CMU 15-281 warehouse-inspired reconstruction; changed dimensions
Three labelled robots occupy distinct cells of a 12-cell warehouse. Count legal joint positions and compare with the unconstrained product count.
---SOLUTION---
The first robot has twelve choices, the second eleven and the third ten, giving $12\cdot11\cdot10=1320$ legal joint positions. @@ 39|State legality does not prevent edge-swap collision|CMU-inspired joint-planning reconstruction
Robots occupy adjacent cells $u$ and $v$ and simultaneously exchange positions. The start and end states both have distinct cells. Is the transition valid under an edge-swap prohibition?
---SOLUTION---
No. Both configurations satisfy vertex occupancy constraints, but the transition sends one robot along $u\to v$ while the other traverses the same physical edge in the opposite direction. A model forbidding such simultaneous swaps must reject the joint action. BFS cannot repair a missing legality rule: it will optimally solve whatever graph the transition generator supplies. Joint planning therefore needs both state predicates and transition constraints, rather than independently planned paths checked only at sampled final positions.
---RULE---
Collision-free endpoint states are insufficient when edge swaps are forbidden. Put transition-level conflicts into the successor function before applying shortest-path search.
@@ 40|A complete algorithm choice|Original synthesis
Choose an algorithm for each case: unit-cost grid with unknown solution depth; finite weighted nonnegative road graph; memory-limited finite-branching unit-cost implicit tree; any reachable vertex in a small finite unweighted graph.
---SOLUTION---
BFS is natural for the unit-cost grid and minimizes moves. UCS handles the nonnegative weighted road graph and minimizes total cost with relaxation. IDS suits the memory-limited implicit tree because finite depth bounds terminate each iteration while eventually reaching a finite solution. Either DFS or BFS can establish reachability in the small finite graph; DFS may use a smaller frontier, but its global discovery storage still scales with reachable states. The choice follows the objective and resource assumptions rather than a universal ranking of algorithms.
---RULE---
Choose search by objective, edge assumptions, state-space finiteness and memory constraints. No single uninformed algorithm dominates every task, and reachability is weaker than minimum-cost optimization.
