# Objects, Reference and Precise Information

## 1. Time, prerequisite and the teaching boundary

**Planned teaching time: 60 minutes.** Spend 5 minutes reconnecting sentence structure to meaning, 15 minutes on noun phrases and articles, 10 minutes following worked grammar examples, 12 minutes reading with evidence, 12 minutes listening with transcripts, and 6 minutes on the after-teaching check. These allocations total 60 minutes. If a concept remains unclear, use the next session to repair it rather than rush forward.

This lesson is prepared ahead. It does not establish that you have studied or mastered Sessions 1 and 2. Before teaching progression, their sentence and routine foundations must be secure. No new test is requested from you now. The checks at the end are for use after this lesson has been taught. Future authoring can continue while learner mastery remains unconfirmed.

The target is IELTS Academic. This is a foundation lesson in grammar, reading and listening, not a full IELTS paper and not a claim that these three strands alone constitute the entire examination. A foundation text is deliberately shorter and simpler than an Academic Reading passage. Its purpose is accurate interpretation before length and speed increase.

## 2. Build a noun phrase before building the sentence

A noun names a person, thing, place or concept. In “The student reads a report,” the words student and report are countable nouns: we can identify one student or several students and one report or several reports. A noun phrase may contain a determiner, an adjective and the main noun: “a short report,” “the new room,” or “my university card.” The main noun determines whether the phrase is singular or plural.

In an ordinary complete sentence, a singular countable noun normally needs a determiner. Say “I need a folder,” not “I need folder.” A possessive already supplies a determiner: “my folder” is complete, and “a my folder” is incorrect. An adjective does not supply one: “I need new folder” is still incomplete. The corrected version is “I need a new folder.” Special fixed expressions and headlines exist, but they do not replace the ordinary sentence pattern being learned here.

Use the sentence structure from Session 1. “The folder is blue” has a singular subject and is. “The folders are blue” has a plural subject and are. In present-simple routines, the full noun phrase is the subject: “The new student studies here,” but “The new students study here.” The adjective new does not decide verb agreement. Find the main noun before choosing the verb.

## 3. Singular, plural and nouns that do not take a simple count

Most familiar count nouns add s: room becomes rooms, and card becomes cards. Some endings require es, as in boxes and classes. A consonant before final y often changes to ies: library becomes libraries. A vowel before y normally retains the y: day becomes days. Some nouns are irregular: child becomes children, and the usual everyday plural of person is people. Learn the form of each new word rather than assuming every noun follows one spelling rule. Endings such as o and f have exceptions, so a short beginner rule is not a universal spelling algorithm.

Compare “one computer” and “three computers.” A number greater than one normally requires the plural form of a count noun. “Three computer” is therefore an error even if the listener can guess the meaning. In a completion answer, an omitted plural ending can also make the completed sentence grammatically wrong. The word before a gap may tell you what form to listen or read for.

In their ordinary material or general-information senses, information and equipment are uncountable. Say “some information” and “some equipment,” not “an information” or “three equipments.” To count units, name a countable unit: “a piece of information,” “two items of equipment.” The main noun in “two pieces of information” is pieces, so it takes plural agreement. Some nouns change countability with meaning; this lesson teaches the stated everyday senses and does not classify every possible use of a word.

## 4. Choose a, an, the or no article for a stated reason

Use a or an with a singular countable noun when introducing one member of a type without identifying it as already known: “Mira needs a notebook.” The choice between a and an depends on the first sound of the following word, not its first written letter. Say “an old notebook” because old starts with a vowel sound, but “a useful notebook” because useful begins with a consonant sound like the start of you. Say “a university card” and “an hour.” The first written letter is therefore an unreliable shortcut.

Use the when the context identifies which referent is meant. A second mention often supplies that identification: “Mira buys a notebook. The notebook is blue.” The article the can also identify something without a previous sentence when a description or shared context makes it clear: “the information desk beside the entrance.” It does not mean that every second noun automatically takes the. The listener must be able to identify the intended referent.

For general statements about an entire type, plural count nouns and uncountable nouns often have no article: “Libraries provide information.” For a specific group, use the: “The libraries in this study are open at weekends.” Removing the changes the scope. Similarly, “I need a report” leaves the particular report unspecified, while “I need the report on this desk” identifies one. An article is a meaning choice, not a decoration added after the sentence is written.

Use this/that for one referent and these/those for plural referents: “this room,” “those rooms.” Distance may be physical or discourse-related. “These equipment” is not a standard phrase in the ordinary uncountable sense; “this equipment” is. The reference laboratory below uses only regular countable examples so that its visible contrasts are unambiguous.

## 5. Follow reference across sentences

Read: “A tutor opens a box. The box contains three notebooks. They are blue.” The first a box introduces one box; the box refers back to it. In the last sentence they naturally refers to the three notebooks, not to one box. Number agreement supplies evidence, while the wider meaning still matters.

Now compare: “Two students bring notebooks. They leave them on the desk.” The subject pronoun they refers to the students, while the object pronoun them refers to the notebooks. Both groups are plural, so number alone cannot resolve the reference; the actions and sentence roles do. Reading precisely means using syntax and context together instead of attaching a pronoun to whichever noun is nearest.

## 6. Ten fully explained grammar examples

1. **Correct “I need pen.”** Write “I need a pen.” The noun pen is singular and countable, and no possessive or other determiner has already been supplied. Adding only an adjective would not fix the missing determiner.
2. **Choose the article in “She carries ___ umbrella.”** Use an because umbrella begins with a vowel sound. The object is newly introduced, so an introduces one unspecified member of its type.
3. **Explain “a useful idea.”** The adjective useful begins with the consonant sound found at the start of you. That sound determines a, even though the noun idea itself begins with a vowel sound. Article choice follows the next pronounced word.
4. **Correct “an university card.”** Write “a university card.” University begins with a consonant sound in standard pronunciation. Do not decide from the written u alone.
5. **Complete the second mention.** “A visitor brings a bag. ___ bag is heavy.” Use the because the previous sentence identifies which bag. The second sentence is not introducing another unspecified bag.
6. **Correct “The three library is open.”** Write “The three libraries are open.” The number requires a plural noun, its consonant-y spelling changes to ies, and the plural subject requires are. One local correction cannot leave the other agreement errors behind.
7. **Correct “I need an information.”** Write “I need some information” for an unspecified amount, or “I need a piece of information” for one countable unit. The noun's ordinary uncountable use does not take a/an directly.
8. **Explain “Libraries help students.”** The sentence discusses the types in general, so the plural nouns need no article. “The libraries help the students” would instead point to contextually identified groups.
9. **Resolve a pronoun.** “A librarian checks two cards. They are valid.” They refers to the cards: it is plural, and validity is the described property. The singular librarian would require a singular personal pronoun.
10. **Correct “This folders belongs to Mina.”** Write “These folders belong to Mina.” The plural noun requires these and the present-simple plural subject takes belong. Alternatively “This folder belongs to Mina” is correct when exactly one folder is intended; that changes the number meaning.

## 7. Reading: a small study centre, with evidence for every answer

The following original passage is independent teaching material, not an official IELTS text. Read for referents, exact quantities and missing information. First follow the worked examples; use the later check only after teaching.

**Line 1.** Cedar Study Centre has an information desk and two study rooms.

**Line 2.** The desk is beside the entrance. A librarian works there on weekdays.

**Line 3.** The study rooms contain six computers in total, with three in each room.

**Line 4.** Students bring a university card when they use a computer.

**Line 5.** A short workshop takes place every Wednesday. The workshop lasts an hour.

**Line 6.** The centre provides notebooks for the workshop, but students bring their own pens.

**Line 7.** Visitors can use the study rooms, but only registered students can borrow books.

**Line 8.** The passage gives no information about charges for computer use.

**Worked True/False example.** “Each study room contains six computers” is False. Line 3 explicitly allocates three to each room; six is the combined total. Replacing the quantifier each with in total changes the proposition.

**Worked Not Given example.** “Computer use is free” is Not Given. Neither a fee nor a zero fee is stated, and Line 8 makes the absence explicit. A plausible real-world expectation cannot supply textual evidence. Not Given does not mean that the statement has been proved false.

**Worked reference example.** In Line 5, the workshop refers back to the short workshop introduced in the previous sentence. An hour describes its duration. The day and duration are different requested facts and should not be exchanged in an answer.

**Worked one-word completion.** “Students supply their own ___ for the workshop.” With the instruction ONE WORD ONLY, answer pens. Line 6 distinguishes what the centre supplies from what students bring. “Their own pens” contains three words and exceeds this task's stated limit, although it expresses the same meaning.

When explaining a reading answer, cite its line number and describe the supporting or contradicting phrase. This short passage teaches evidence tracking before the pathway introduces longer Academic Reading arguments.

## 9. Complete summary and error-prevention notes

A noun phrase identifies its main noun, number and reference. A singular countable noun in an ordinary sentence normally needs one appropriate determiner. A/an introduces an unspecified member of a type and follows the next pronounced sound; the marks a contextually identified referent. Plural count nouns or uncountable nouns can refer generally without an article. These choices affect meaning, not just spelling.

Do not combine a/an with my before the same singular noun. Do not add s mechanically to every word: learn libraries, children and people. Do not treat the ordinary uncountable nouns information and equipment as direct counts. Use a stated unit when a number is intended. Identify the main noun before choosing is/are or a present-simple verb form.

In reading, trace reference through grammar and context, preserve the difference between each and in total, and distinguish contradiction from absent information. In listening, follow a correction to its final value, attach adjectives to the right object, and identify the requested answer type before writing. A correct idea can still violate a completion word limit or omit a necessary plural ending.

These rules are a foundation, not a claim that article use has been exhausted. Institutional expressions, abstract nouns, geographic names and more advanced reference patterns require later lessons with their own contexts. The current objective is to explain the decisions made here accurately before extending them.
