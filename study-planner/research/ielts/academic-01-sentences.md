# Session 1. Build a sentence, find evidence, and hear a correction

**Track:** IELTS Academic foundations. **Planned study time: 60 minutes.** **Entry level:** no previous grammar knowledge is assumed. **Status:** first teaching draft, available for study and review. This lesson starts the route to Academic Reading and Listening; it is not a full IELTS test or an IELTS band prediction.

## 1. Your timetable and learning targets

Read the teaching before attempting the independent check. The worked examples are instruction: read the answers as you go. Save the independent check until the final block. A difficult section may need a second session. Do not rush an unclear rule to meet the clock.

The vocabulary is deliberately small. Grammar, reading, and listening reuse the same meanings so that understanding one skill supports the others. More demanding academic texts and natural multi-speaker recordings come later. The full IELTS examination also has Writing and Speaking; this requested pathway currently teaches Grammar, Reading, and Listening.

## 2. Start with meaning: a subject and a description

A **sentence** expresses a complete idea in context. In the statements taught here, the **subject** names the person or thing we are describing. The **verb** changes to match the subject. The **complement** supplies an identity, a condition, or a location. We use a small set of forms of the verb **be**: **am, is, are**.

In **Emma is a student**, *Emma* is the subject, *is* is the verb, and *a student* describes Emma's identity. Without that final phrase, *Emma is* normally leaves this intended meaning unfinished. In **We are ready**, *ready* describes our condition. In **We are in room fourteen**, *in room fourteen* describes our location. These are three different kinds of complement; do not assume that every complement is a person or an object.

An English statement usually places these parts in this order:

**Subject → matching form of be → complement.**

This is a pattern for the present-tense statements in this lesson, not a definition of every English sentence. Later lessons introduce other verbs, questions, imperatives, complex clauses, and different tenses. For now, learn this useful pattern thoroughly.

### Pronouns replace names

A **pronoun** can stand for a person or thing that the context identifies. **I** means the speaker. **You** means the listener or listeners. **We** includes the speaker and at least one other person. **They** refers here to more than one person or thing. **He**, **she**, and **it** are singular in the beginner examples below. Singular *they* also exists in English, but it still takes **are**, and its fuller uses will be taught separately. Do not infer a person's pronoun from a name alone; our examples explicitly establish their referents.

| Subject | Form of be | Complete example | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | am | I am a student. | The speaker identifies themself as a student. |
| You | are | You are ready. | The listener or listeners are prepared. |
| He | is | He is a teacher. | The identified person is a teacher. |
| She | is | She is a student. | The identified person is a student. |
| It | is | It is ready. | The identified thing is prepared. |
| We | are | We are ready. | The speaker and another person or group are prepared. |
| They | are | They are students. | The identified people are students. |

**You are** is correct for one listener and for several listeners. It does not become *you is* when there is only one person. A person's name normally takes **is** when it refers to one person: **Emma is ready.** Two people joined by *and* take **are** in our examples: **Emma and Alex are ready.**

### Three small vocabulary rules

1. **A student** and **a teacher** name one person in a role. In this pattern, an ordinary singular countable noun needs an appropriate determiner, such as **a**. Write **I am a student**, not *I am student*. **Students** is plural: **They are students**, not *They are a students*.
2. **Ready** and **late** are adjectives. They describe a condition. Write **We are ready**, not *We are a ready*. **Late** means arriving after the expected time; **not late** denies lateness. It does not by itself prove that someone arrived early rather than exactly on time.
3. **In room fourteen** is a location phrase. *In* tells us that someone is inside the room. The room number identifies the room, so **room fourteen** does not mean fourteen separate rooms.

Use a capital letter at the start of a sentence, capitalise names, and always write the pronoun **I** as a capital letter. End a statement with a full stop and a direct question with a question mark. Punctuation does not repair a wrong verb: *I is ready.* remains incorrect.

## 3. Affirmative statements: four worked examples

### Worked example 1. Choose the verb by the subject

**Task:** Complete “I ___ ready.”

**Answer:** **I am ready.** The subject is **I**, so the present form taught here is **am**. *Ready* is an adjective, so no article is required. *I is ready* uses the wrong agreement. *I are ready* also uses the wrong agreement. Do not choose the verb by the word after the gap; choose it by the subject.

### Worked example 2. A name and a plural group

**Task:** Complete “Emma ___ a student. Emma and Alex ___ students.”

**Answer:** **Emma is a student. Emma and Alex are students.** The first subject identifies one person and takes **is**. The second names two people and takes **are**. The complement changes from singular *a student* to plural *students* because it identifies the two people as members of that role. The verb and the noun have different grammatical jobs; changing the noun alone does not fix agreement.

### Worked example 3. Explain a minimal correction

**Task:** Repair “You is a teacher.” Assume you are addressing one teacher.

**Answer:** **You are a teacher.** The pronoun **you** takes **are**, including when it refers to one person. The complement **a teacher** is already appropriate for that one person. Replacing it with *teachers* would alter the intended number without fixing the actual verb error. Repair the element that violates the rule while preserving the intended meaning.

### Worked example 4. Identity and condition

**Task:** Repair “I am student. We are a ready.”

**Answer:** **I am a student. We are ready.** *Student* is a singular countable role noun and needs a determiner here. *Ready* is an adjective and does not take **a** in this construction. Both sentences already have the correct form of **be**. This example shows why a blanket rule such as “put a after be” is wrong: the kind of complement determines the article pattern.

## 4. Negatives: deny a description precisely

Place **not** immediately after **am, is, or are**. The subject and complement remain in their original positions.

| Affirmative | Negative | What changes |
|---|---|---|
| I am late. | I am not late. | The speaker denies being late. |
| Emma is a teacher. | Emma is not a teacher. | Emma's stated role is denied. |
| We are in room fourteen. | We are not in room fourteen. | That location is denied; the actual location is not supplied. |

Negation does not tell us every alternative. **Emma is not a teacher** does not prove that Emma is a student. Emma could have another role. Likewise, **We are not in room fourteen** does not identify our room. This distinction matters both in grammar and in reading evidence.

### Worked example 5. Negate without inventing information

**Task:** Make “Alex is ready” negative and state what you can infer.

**Answer:** **Alex is not ready.** Insert **not** after **is**. This denies readiness at the relevant time. It does not say why Alex is not ready, when Alex will be ready, or whether Alex is late. *Alex not is ready* has incorrect word order. *Alex does not is ready* incorrectly introduces **does** into this present construction with **be** as the main verb.

## 5. Yes–no questions: move be before the subject

A **yes–no question** asks whether a description is true. Move **am, is, or are** before the subject. Keep the complement after the subject.

| Statement | Question | Positive short answer | Negative short answer |
|---|---|---|---|
| I am late. | Am I late? | Yes, you are. | No, you are not. |
| You are ready. | Are you ready? | Yes, I am. | No, I am not. |
| Emma is a student. | Is Emma a student? | Yes, she is. | No, she is not. |
| Alex is a teacher. | Is Alex a teacher? | Yes, he is. | No, he is not. |
| It is ready. | Is it ready? | Yes, it is. | No, it is not. |
| We are late. | Are we late? | Yes, we are. | No, we are not. |
| They are ready. | Are they ready? | Yes, they are. | No, they are not. |

For this table, Emma uses **she** and Alex uses **he**. The **we** answer assumes the respondent belongs to the same group as the questioner. In another conversation, a teacher outside the group might answer **Yes, you are**. Pronouns describe the actual speaker, listener, and group; they are not mechanical copies of the question.

### Worked example 6. Turn a statement into a question

**Task:** Ask whether Emma is in room fourteen.

**Answer:** **Is Emma in room fourteen?** Begin with **is**, then write the subject **Emma**, then the location phrase. *Does Emma is in room fourteen?* is incorrect because this main-verb **be** question does not use **does**. *Is in room fourteen Emma?* places the complement before the subject and is not the neutral pattern taught here.

### Worked example 7. Answer from the correct person's viewpoint

**Task:** A teacher asks you “Are you a student?” You are a student. Give a short answer.

**Answer:** **Yes, I am.** In the teacher's question, **you** means you, the listener. In your answer, **I** means you, now the speaker. The verb changes from **are** to **am** because the subject changes. **Yes, you are** would describe the teacher, not answer the teacher's question about you. This is a change of viewpoint, not an exception to agreement.

## 6. Contractions, spelling, and an information question

A **contraction** combines words in a shorter written form. An apostrophe marks omitted letters. It is not a decoration, and it is different from a comma. In the examples below, **'s** means **is**; later you will learn other uses of **'s**.

| Full form | Common contracted form |
|---|---|
| I am | I'm |
| You are | You're |
| He is / She is / It is | He's / She's / It's |
| We are / They are | We're / They're |
| I am not | I'm not |
| She is not | She isn't / She's not |
| They are not | They aren't / They're not |

These forms are common in conversation and informal writing. The present standard beginner form is **I'm not**, not *I amn't*. A positive short answer at the end of the clause normally uses the full verb: **Yes, I am**, not *Yes, I'm*. Negative short answers can use contractions: **No, she isn't** and **No, I'm not** are correct.

### Worked example 8. A positive and a negative short answer

**Task:** Repair “Yes, she's” as a complete positive answer to “Is Emma ready?” Then give a negative answer using a contraction.

**Answer:** **Yes, she is.** The positive short answer ends with the full verb. **No, she isn't** is a correct negative answer, and **No, she's not** is another. **She's ready** is a correct full statement, because **ready** follows the contraction. Do not turn the rule into “contractions are always wrong”: the clause position and construction matter.

### Worked example 9. Distinguish similar-looking words

**Task:** Repair “Your ready. Their students.”

**Answer:** **You're ready. They're students.** **You're** means **you are** and **they're** means **they are**. **Your** is used for possession, as in *your class*. **Their** is also possessive, as in *their class*. Each faulty sentence is missing the required verb. These spellings may sound similar in some accents, so in writing you must check the meaning, not just the sound. Possession will receive fuller treatment in another session.

### Worked example 10. Ask for the missing name

**Task:** A form has an empty name field. Ask the person for their name using **what**.

**Answer:** **What is your name?** **What** asks for information rather than a yes or no. **Your name** is the subject of this question, and **is** agrees with singular **name**. **What's your name?** is the common contracted version. In the answer **My name is Emma**, **my** means belonging to the speaker. Asking **Is your name Emma?** is a different question: it checks a proposed name rather than requesting an unknown one.

## 7. Read an original passage with evidence

### Before reading: six useful words

**Class** means a lesson or a group studying together, depending on context. **Teacher** means a person who teaches. **Student** means a person who studies. **Room** means a space in a building. **Together** means with one another. **Ready** means prepared. In the passage, *class* refers to this teaching session, and *we* means Emma and Sam. Alex uses **he**, and Emma uses **she** in our exercises.

### Original passage: A first class

> [1] My name is Emma. I am a student.
>
> [2] Sam is a student too. We are in room fourteen.
>
> [3] Alex is our teacher. He is ready.
>
> [4] The room is small, but it is quiet.
>
> [5] We are not late. We are ready for our first class.

**Too** means “also” here. **Our** means belonging to or associated with our group. **Small** describes size. **Quiet** means there is little noise. **But** joins two descriptions that the writer contrasts: the room is small, yet it has a useful quiet condition. **For our first class** explains what Emma and Sam are ready for. You do not need to know a student's nationality, age, or degree to understand this passage.

### Line-by-line meaning

**Line 1** names the speaker and identifies their role. **My name** takes **is** because *name* is singular; **I** takes **am** in the next sentence. These are different subjects, even though both sentences describe the same person.

**Line 2** says that Sam also studies. **We** refers to Emma and Sam together, so **are** is required. The line explicitly supplies their room. It does not state that Alex is physically in that room at the same moment.

**Line 3** establishes Alex's role and says that he is ready. **Our** associates the teacher with the student group. It does not mean that the students own the teacher. Reference depends on the context: **he** refers to Alex here because the passage explicitly establishes that use.

**Line 4** gives two properties of the room. **It** refers to the room, not to Alex. Both descriptions can be true at once; small size and low noise do not contradict each other. The conjunction **but** indicates the writer's contrast, not logical negation.

**Line 5** denies lateness and states readiness. It does not state the start time. Therefore, you cannot calculate when the class begins or infer that the students arrived exactly ten minutes early.

### Three complete evidence demonstrations

**Statement A: Emma and Sam are students.** This is **supported**. Line 1 identifies Emma as a student, and line 2 does the same for Sam. Both pieces of evidence are needed to support the combined claim. Copying only the room number would not answer the question about their roles.

**Statement B: Alex is a student in this class rather than its teacher.** This is **contradicted** by line 3, which explicitly calls Alex the teacher. The statement proposes the wrong role in this described class. Do not confuse an explicit contradiction with silence about an unrelated fact.

**Statement C: Emma is nineteen years old.** This is **not given**. The passage identifies Emma's name and role but does not give an age. A student may be nineteen or another age. Missing evidence is not evidence that the student is not nineteen. In IELTS information-identification tasks, this supported/contradicted/unstated distinction underlies **True / False / Not Given**. Use the text rather than assumptions about students. [Official Academic Reading format](https://ielts.org/take-a-test/test-types/ielts-academic-test/ielts-academic-format-reading).

## 9. Complete summary and common traps

1. **Choose be from the subject.** I takes am; he, she, it, and the singular names here take is; you, we, and they take are.
2. **Keep the description complete.** An identity, condition, or location follows be in these statements. Do not stop an intended statement at “Emma is.”
3. **You takes are even for one listener.** The number of people represented by you does not license “you is.”
4. **A singular countable role needs an appropriate determiner here.** “A student” is singular; “students” is plural. Adjectives such as ready do not take a in this pattern.
5. **Negate by inserting not after be.** “Is not a teacher” denies that identity without proving a different identity.
6. **Ask a yes–no question by moving be before the subject.** Do not add do or does when be is the main verb in this present construction.
7. **Answer from the respondent's viewpoint.** A question with you can require an answer with I. Check who each pronoun refers to.
8. **Use the full verb at the end of a positive short answer.** “Yes, I am” is complete; “Yes, I'm” is not the standard positive short answer. Negative contractions are different.
9. **An apostrophe can signal omitted letters.** You're means you are; your is possessive. They're means they are; their is possessive. Sound alone is insufficient when writing.
10. **Use evidence separately for every reading claim.** Supported, contradicted, and unstated are three different outcomes. An unstated age is not a false age.
11. **A listening suggestion may be rejected.** A proposed number or name is not automatically the final answer. Track the response and correction.
12. **Respect the requested field and word limit.** A room number does not answer a name field, and a teacher's name does not answer the student's name field. The exercises here are small demonstrations, not full-length IELTS questions.

### How we decide whether to advance

Do not treat a score alone as proof of complete learning. You should be able to reconstruct all three sentence forms for I, a singular name, and we; explain each change; distinguish contradiction from missing evidence; and recognise a corrected name or number without reading its transcript during the attempt. Record which items you attempted independently, which used a hint, and which still need explanation. If any foundation remains unclear, revisit that section and use a new example in the next session before advancing. Closing this page, opening all answers, or selecting a correct laboratory form does not mark the lesson mastered.
