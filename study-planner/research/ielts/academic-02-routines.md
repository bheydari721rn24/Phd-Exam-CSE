## 2. From descriptions to routines

In Session1, “Maya is a student” connects a subject to a description. The verb *is* is a form of *be*. In “Maya studies biology,” the word *studies* expresses her activity or course of study. Both sentences describe present circumstances, but their grammar is different.

A **routine** is something that happens repeatedly, such as visiting a library every Monday. A **state** is a condition or situation, such as knowing a word. The present simple can express regular activities, general facts and states viewed as current. It does not mean that something must remain true forever. “Maya lives near the library” describes her current home; it does not promise that she will never move.

The basic statement order is **subject + present-simple verb + other information**. For the verbs practiced here, use the base form with *I, you, we,* and *they*. Use the third-person singular form with *he, she,* and *it*, or a single person or thing named directly.

| Subject | Verb | Complete statement | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| I | study | I study biology. | The speaker studies the subject. |
| You | study | You study biology. | The listener studies the subject. |
| We | study | We study biology. | A group including the speaker studies it. |
| They | study | They study biology. | Another group studies it. |
| She | studies | She studies biology. | One female person studies it. |
| He | studies | He studies biology. | One male person studies it. |
| The library | opens | The library opens at nine. | One institution has a stated opening time. |

Do not write “She is studies biology.” That combines the present-simple main verb with an unnecessary form of *be*. “She is studying biology” is a different, continuous construction, taught later. Here our target is the simple form “She studies biology.”

## 3. Third-person forms: spelling, meaning and sound

Most practiced verbs add **-s**: *read → reads, work → works, open → opens*. A verb ending in consonant plus **y** usually changes **y** to **ies**: *study → studies*. A vowel before **y** keeps the letter: *play → plays*. Common verbs in this lesson include *go → goes, do → does,* and *have → has*. Learn these as full forms rather than applying one spelling rule to every word.

For common endings such as **-s, -sh, -ch, -x** and **-z**, the third-person spelling normally uses **-es**: *wash → washes, watch → watches, fix → fixes*. The spelling and the pronunciation are related but not identical. An ending may sound like /s/ in *works*, /z/ in *reads*, or an extra /ɪz/ syllable in *watches*. Do not assume that every written final **s** adds another syllable.

This matters for listening because “the classes start” and “the class starts” have different subjects and agreement. A quiet final sound may carry grammatical information even when the main vocabulary is familiar. At this stage, recognize the distinction in the teaching audio; later sessions will develop connected-speech discrimination with more voices.

The form follows the grammatical subject, not the nearest noun. “The student with two books studies here” has singular subject *the student*. The phrase *with two books* does not make the subject plural. “Maya and Leo study here” has a coordinated plural subject and uses the base form.

## 4. Negatives: do not and does not

For the ordinary main verbs in this lesson, build a negative with **subject + do/does + not + base verb**. Use **does** with a third-person singular subject and **do** with the others. In ordinary writing and speech, **do not** contracts to **don't**, and **does not** to **doesn't**.

“Maya studies on Monday” becomes “Maya does not study on Monday.” The third-person information appears in **does**, so the following verb returns to its base form **study**. “Maya doesn't studies” marks that information twice and is wrong in this construction.

| Statement | Negative | Explanation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| They work here. | They do not work here. | The plural subject selects *do*. |
| Leo works here. | Leo does not work here. | *Does* carries the singular agreement. |
| She has a card. | She does not have a card. | The main verb returns from *has* to *have*. |
| The library opens early. | The library does not open early. | One institution selects *does*. |

Keep the earlier *be* pattern separate. “Maya is not late” uses **is + not**. Do not change it to “Maya does not is late.” The present-simple main verbs practiced here need *do* support; present *be* uses its own negative and question pattern. This explanation covers the current constructions, not every special use of *do*, modal verbs or emphatic English.

## 5. Questions, short answers and one important exception

For a yes/no question about these main verbs, put **do/does before the subject**, then use the base verb: “Does Maya study here?” The answer is “Yes, she does” or “No, she doesn't.” A short answer uses the auxiliary that matches the question; “Yes, she is” would answer a different kind of question.

For an information question, add a question word before the auxiliary: “Where does Maya study?” asks for a place; “When does the library open?” asks for a time; “What does Leo read?” asks for an object. The word order is question word + auxiliary + subject + base verb.

There is an important subject-question exception. In “Who studies here?”, *who* asks for the subject itself, so this ordinary positive subject question does not use **does**. Compare “Who does Maya teach?” Here *Maya* is already the subject and *who* asks for the object, so *does* is required. The distinction concerns grammatical roles rather than a rule that the word *who* always behaves one way.

## 6. Ten fully worked grammar examples

### Example1: explain the ending

**Task:** Complete “Maya ___ biology” with *study*. **Worked answer:** “Maya studies biology.” The subject names one person, equivalent to *she*, so a third-person singular main verb is required. Because *study* ends in consonant plus **y**, the spelling changes to *studies*. The ending expresses agreement; it does not mean that she is studying at this exact second.

### Example2: repair double agreement

**Task:** Repair “Leo doesn't reads books.” **Worked answer:** “Leo doesn't read books.” The auxiliary *doesn't* already carries singular agreement. The main verb must be the base *read*. Removing only the negative would change the meaning; changing *reads* to *read* repairs grammar while retaining the denial.

### Example3: ask about a place

**Task:** Ask for the place in “They study at the library.” **Worked answer:** “Where do they study?” The unknown element is a place, so use *where*. The subject is plural, so select *do*. Keep the subject after the auxiliary and the base verb after the subject. “Where they study?” lacks the ordinary direct-question auxiliary in this context.

### Example4: keep the two verb systems apart

**Task:** Compare “Is Maya ready?” with “Does Maya study here?” **Worked explanation:** *Ready* is an adjective complement, so the first question uses *be*. *Study* is an ordinary main verb, so the second uses *does* support. The answers are “Yes, she is” and “Yes, she does” respectively. One auxiliary cannot be substituted for the other merely because both questions begin with a capital letter.

### Example5: a plural name pair

**Task:** Complete “Maya and Leo ___ here” with *work*. **Worked answer:** “Maya and Leo work here.” The subject coordinates two people and corresponds to *they*. Singular-looking individual names do not make the whole coordinated subject singular. The base form is required.

### Example6: a subject question

**Task:** Ask for the person in “Maya opens the library.” **Worked answer:** “Who opens the library?” The missing person is the subject. Keep the verb form appropriate to this ordinary singular interpretation of *who* and do not add an unnecessary *does*. This differs from asking what Maya opens, where Maya remains the subject.

### Example7: a negative with an irregular verb

**Task:** Make “She has a card” negative. **Worked answer:** “She does not have a card.” The statement uses irregular *has*. In the negative, *does* carries the agreement and the main verb is the base *have*. “She doesn't has” keeps an inflected form where a base is needed.

### Example8: frequency and be

**Task:** Place *usually* in “They arrive early” and “They are early.” **Worked answers:** “They usually arrive early” and “They are usually early.” In these ordinary neutral statements, the frequency adverb normally precedes the main lexical verb but follows present *be*. Other placements can serve emphasis in wider English; these are the neutral patterns to learn first.

### Example9: interpret a timetable

**Task:** Explain “The lesson starts at nine.” **Worked explanation:** A scheduled event can use the present simple even if the particular occurrence is later. The time phrase supplies the relevant timetable information. The sentence is not evidence that an event is currently in progress, and *starts* agrees with singular *lesson*.

### Example10: locate the real subject

**Task:** Choose the verb in “The student with two notebooks ___ here.” **Worked answer:** “studies.” The head of the subject phrase is singular *student*. The plural *notebooks* belongs inside a descriptive prepositional phrase. Agreement follows the subject head rather than the last noun before the verb.

## 7. Reading: identify the proposition and its evidence

**Original teaching passage.** This deliberately short passage prepares evidence tracking before longer academic texts. It is not taken from an official IELTS paper.

> **Line1.** The university library opens at nine every weekday.
>
> **Line2.** Maya studies biology. She makes two library visits each week, on Mondays and Thursdays.
>
> **Line3.** Her friend Leo studies history, but he does not visit the library on Thursdays.
>
> **Line4.** He usually reads at home on that day.
>
> **Line5.** Students use their university cards to borrow books.
>
> **Line6.** The library does not charge students for its short introductory tour.
>
> **Line7.** The tour starts at ten on Tuesday and lasts thirty minutes.
>
> **Line8.** A student assistant leads the tour; the passage does not state the assistant's name.

Before answering, break a statement into **who + action/state + qualification**. A matching noun alone does not establish the whole proposition. For example, a sentence mentioning Leo and Thursday can say that he visits or that he does not visit; the negative reverses the central claim.

**Worked statement1:** “Maya visits the library twice during the stated weekly pattern.” **Answer: True.** Line2 supplies two named weekly days. The word *twice* is a paraphrase of that listed frequency, not a phrase that must appear exactly. This refers to the described routine; it does not assert that no unusual extra visit could ever occur.

**Worked statement2:** “Leo visits the library on Thursdays.” **Answer: False.** Line3 explicitly says he does not. This is a contradiction, not missing information. The presence of *does not* is decisive even though the names and day match.

**Worked statement3:** “The introductory tour costs students five pounds.” **Answer: False.** Line6 states that students are not charged for that tour. The exact amount five pounds is contradicted by the zero-charge statement.

**Worked statement4:** “The student assistant is called Sara.” **Answer: Not Given.** Line8 expressly leaves the name unstated. Do not invent an assistant's identity or treat an unsupported detail as false simply because it cannot be found. It could be true or false outside the text; the passage does not decide it.

**Worked statement5:** “The library opens on Saturday.” **Answer: Not Given.** Line1 gives weekday opening information. It does not state weekend hours or say that weekday openings are the only openings. Inferring “closed on Saturday” from silence would introduce an unsupported exclusivity claim.

**Worked completion:** “The tour begins at ___ on Tuesday.” If the practice instruction allows **ONE WORD**, write **ten**. The evidence is Line7; *Tuesday* answers the day rather than the time. If a different task permits a number, follow that task's exact instruction. A correct fact in the wrong field or an answer exceeding the stated limit can still be unacceptable.

**Reference tracking:** In Line4, *he* refers to Leo because Line3 introduced Leo as the relevant male person. *That day* refers to Thursday. Read across sentence boundaries before attaching a pronoun to the nearest unrelated noun. This small habit becomes essential in longer academic passages.

**Vocabulary from context:** *Borrow* means taking something for temporary use, normally with an expectation of return; it does not mean buying it. *Lasts thirty minutes* describes duration, while *starts at ten* gives a clock time. *Free* can mean without charge in a service context, but its meaning depends on context elsewhere. Learn each word in its complete proposition.

## 9. Summary and important examination habits

The present-simple statement for the main verbs practiced here uses a third-person singular form with one person or thing. A negative or ordinary object question places that agreement in *does* and returns the main verb to its base. Present *be* follows a different pattern. A subject question such as “Who studies here?” does not follow the ordinary object-question pattern.

In reading, compare complete propositions. A repeated noun is not enough; attend to the person, action, negative and time qualification. Use *False* for an explicit contradiction and *Not Given* when the text does not decide the statement. Weekend hours cannot be inferred from a weekday statement alone. In listening, separate provisional information from a final correction, clock times from duration, and one person's routine from another's.

Keep each answer tied to the current task. A reading passage and an independent listening script may describe different schedules. Observe the exact answer instruction, including word or number limits. Spelling, a correct fact and a correct field are separate checks. These habits are taught gradually here; the short exercises do not establish readiness for an advanced IELTS score.
