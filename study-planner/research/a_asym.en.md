# Asymptotic Notation and Growth Comparison

*Data Structures and Algorithms · Chapter 2 · Approved English chapter · 18 fully worked problems*

## 1. Scope, prerequisites, and four reviewed university texts

This chapter turns an operation-count function into a defensible statement about its growth. It teaches the precise quantifiers behind upper, lower, tight, and strict bounds; constructive witnesses and disproof; limits and their failure modes; a proved hierarchy of logarithms, powers, exponentials, and factorials; and safe algebra with asymptotic relations. The previous chapter fixed input size and cost model. The next chapters will derive costs from loops and solve recurrences. Those techniques are not silently assumed here. Our default domain is positive integers <span class="math-inline">n</span> tending to infinity, and functions are eventually nonnegative unless a signed variant is expressly given. Elementary algebra, logarithms, induction, and limits are prerequisites; each needed fact is restated when used.

The selected written sources were **actually read in their chapter-relevant pages**: MIT 6.006 Recitation 1 (PDF pp. 3 and 6), Stanford CS161 Lecture 2 (pp. 6–7), Carnegie Mellon 15-451 Lecture 2 (pp. 10–11), and Kevin Wayne's Princeton algorithm-analysis slides (pp. 3–6). MIT and Stanford supply precise eventual bounds and witness methods; CMU supplies strict notation and oscillation; Princeton supplies algebraic laws, limit proofs, and parameter warnings. They were selected from a bounded pool, documented in the source audit, rather than assumed to be the four globally best courses. The source audit is part of the development record; complete public citations are at the end of this lesson. The exercises below are newly worded; a label saying *inspired by* identifies a reasoning pattern, not a copied question.

## 2. The mathematical contract behind a complexity claim

### 2.1 Which function is being bounded?

Let <span class="math-inline">T(x)</span> be the number of charged primitive operations executed on an instance <span class="math-inline">x</span>. If <span class="math-inline">|x|=n</span> is the specified input-size measure, the worst-case resource function is <span class="math-inline">T<sub>worst</sub>(n)=max<sub>|x|=n</sub>T(x)</span> when the maximum and at least one such input exist. A claim such as <span class="math-inline">T<sub>worst</sub> ∈ O(n²)</span> concerns **that function** under **that model and encoding**. It does not say every input takes quadratic time, nor does a bound on one algorithm automatically apply to every algorithm for the problem. For an empty size class use a stated alternative, such as a supremum over inputs of size at most <span class="math-inline">n</span>. For average-case cost, first specify an input distribution. For randomized algorithms, specify whether the claim bounds worst-case time over random choices, expectation, or a high-probability event.

Asymptotics ignore a *fixed* initial segment of sizes. They do not declare a small-input run free or a large constant irrelevant in practice. Consider two hypothetical implementations with costs <span class="math-inline">A(n)=1000n</span> and <span class="math-inline">B(n)=n²</span>. Their symbolic growth classes are <span class="math-inline">Θ(n)</span> and <span class="math-inline">Θ(n²)</span>, but <span class="math-inline">B(n)&lt;A(n)</span> for <span class="math-inline">1&lt;n&lt;1000</span>. The crossover occurs at <span class="math-inline">n=1000</span>. It is a modelling example, not a measured benchmark.

### 2.2 Function classes are sets

For an eventually nonnegative comparison function <span class="math-inline">g</span>, <span class="math-inline">O(g)</span> is a **set of functions**. Writing <span class="math-inline">f∈O(g)</span> is formally precise. The conventional notation <span class="math-inline">f=O(g)</span> is read as membership, not ordinary equality; it cannot be algebraically reversed to infer equality of functions. Throughout, choose <span class="math-inline">c,c<sub>1</sub>,c<sub>2</sub>&gt;0</span> and an integer threshold <span class="math-inline">n<sub>0</sub></span> independent of <span class="math-inline">n</span>. A constant may depend on fixed parameters such as a prescribed exponent, but not on the input size it is supposed to bound.

<div class="formula-block formula-steps"><div>f ∈ O(g) ⇔ ∃c&gt;0, n<sub>0</sub> : ∀n≥n<sub>0</sub>, 0≤f(n)≤c g(n).</div><div>f ∈ Ω(g) ⇔ ∃c&gt;0, n<sub>0</sub> : ∀n≥n<sub>0</sub>, 0≤c g(n)≤f(n).</div><div>f ∈ Θ(g) ⇔ ∃c<sub>1</sub>,c<sub>2</sub>&gt;0,n<sub>0</sub> : ∀n≥n<sub>0</sub>, 0≤c<sub>1</sub>g(n)≤f(n)≤c<sub>2</sub>g(n).</div></div>

The order of quantifiers matters: **one** pair of constants must work for **every** sufficiently large input size. “For infinitely many <span class="math-inline">n</span>” is insufficient. For example, <span class="math-inline">f(n)=n²</span> at odd sizes and <span class="math-inline">f(n)=1</span> at even sizes satisfies <span class="math-inline">f(n)≥n</span> for infinitely many odd sizes, yet <span class="math-inline">f∉Ω(n)</span> because at arbitrarily large even sizes <span class="math-inline">1&lt;cn</span> for every fixed <span class="math-inline">c&gt;0</span>. Exchanging <span class="math-inline">f</span> and <span class="math-inline">g</span> gives <span class="math-inline">f∈Ω(g) ⇔ g∈O(f)</span> when the nonnegativity assumptions hold. Tightness means both directions: <span class="math-inline">Θ(g)=O(g)∩Ω(g)</span>.

### 2.3 Strict relations and asymptotic equivalence

In this subsection assume the comparison function <span class="math-inline">g(n)&gt;0</span> for every sufficiently large <span class="math-inline">n</span>. The little-oh relation is stronger than big-oh:

<div class="formula-block formula-steps"><div>f ∈ o(g) ⇔ ∀ε&gt;0, ∃n<sub>ε</sub> : ∀n≥n<sub>ε</sub>, 0≤f(n)&lt;εg(n).</div><div>f ∈ ω(g) ⇔ ∀M&gt;0, ∃n<sub>M</sub> : ∀n≥n<sub>M</sub>, f(n)&gt;Mg(n)≥0.</div></div>

The strict inequality can be replaced by a non-strict one after changing <span class="math-inline">ε</span> or <span class="math-inline">M</span>; the quantifier over **every** positive constant is decisive. If <span class="math-inline">g(n)&gt;0</span> eventually, then <span class="math-inline">f∈o(g)</span> exactly when <span class="math-inline">f(n)/g(n)→0</span>, and <span class="math-inline">f∈ω(g)</span> exactly when the ratio tends to positive infinity. Also <span class="math-inline">f∈ω(g) ⇔ g∈o(f)</span> when both ratios are eventually defined and positive. Neither <span class="math-inline">o(g)</span> nor <span class="math-inline">ω(g)</span> includes a function in <span class="math-inline">Θ(g)</span>.

Asymptotic equivalence uses a stronger symbol: <span class="math-inline">f∼g</span> means <span class="math-inline">f(n)/g(n)→1</span>, with <span class="math-inline">g(n)≠0</span> eventually. Equivalently, <span class="math-inline">|f−g|/g→0</span> for eventually positive <span class="math-inline">g</span>, or <span class="math-inline">f=g+h</span> with <span class="math-inline">|h|∈o(g)</span>. The residual <span class="math-inline">h</span> may be negative: for example, <span class="math-inline">f(n)=n−1</span> and <span class="math-inline">g(n)=n</span> satisfy <span class="math-inline">f∼g</span> although their difference is <span class="math-inline">−1</span>. This absolute-value formulation respects our nonnegative little-oh convention. Thus <span class="math-inline">f∼g ⇒ f∈Θ(g)</span>; the converse is false: <span class="math-inline">2n∈Θ(n)</span> while <span class="math-inline">2n∼n</span> is false. “Not in <span class="math-inline">Θ(g)</span>” is **not** the same as <span class="math-inline">o(g)</span> or <span class="math-inline">ω(g)</span>; oscillating or incomparable functions need neither strict relation.

For signed mathematical expressions, some texts define <span class="math-inline">f∈O(g)</span> through <span class="math-inline">|f(n)|≤c|g(n)|</span>. This is useful for error terms but is not the same convention as nonnegative runtime bounds. If a polynomial has a negative leading coefficient, it is eventually negative and cannot itself model elapsed time; first determine whether the intended object is an algebraic error or a nonnegative resource. We use the absolute-value convention only when explicitly stated.

## 3. Prove or refute a proposed bound

### 3.1 Construct a witness rather than relying on a slogan

To establish <span class="math-inline">f∈O(g)</span>, exhibit constants <span class="math-inline">c,n<sub>0</sub></span> and verify the inequality for every <span class="math-inline">n≥n<sub>0</sub></span>. For <span class="math-inline">f(n)=3n²+17n+100</span> and <span class="math-inline">n≥1</span>, <span class="math-inline">n≤n²</span> and <span class="math-inline">1≤n²</span>, so <span class="math-inline">f(n)≤120n²</span>. The lower bound is <span class="math-inline">f(n)≥3n²</span>. Consequently <span class="math-inline">f∈Θ(n²)</span> with <span class="math-inline">c<sub>1</sub>=3,c<sub>2</sub>=120,n<sub>0</sub>=1</span>. These constants are valid, not necessarily optimal. A larger threshold can yield much smaller constants.

For an eventual upper bound, finite exceptions are harmless. For example, <span class="math-inline">n²−100n</span> is negative for some positive sizes, so the runtime convention does not apply there; for <span class="math-inline">n≥101</span> it is nonnegative and at most <span class="math-inline">n²</span>. A lower bound also needs a threshold: if <span class="math-inline">n≥200</span>, then <span class="math-inline">n²−100n≥n²/2</span>. This yields an eventual Θ claim for the *eventually nonnegative function*, not a claim about a runtime that is negative on small inputs.

### 3.2 Negating the quantifiers is the cleanest disproof

To refute <span class="math-inline">f∈O(g)</span> with positive <span class="math-inline">g</span>, show that **for every** proposed <span class="math-inline">c&gt;0</span> and <span class="math-inline">n<sub>0</sub></span>, some <span class="math-inline">n≥n<sub>0</sub></span> has <span class="math-inline">f(n)&gt;cg(n)</span>. For <span class="math-inline">f(n)=n²</span> and <span class="math-inline">g(n)=n</span>, choose an integer <span class="math-inline">n&gt;max(c,n<sub>0</sub>)</span>. Then <span class="math-inline">n²&gt;cn</span>. A graph of the first few values cannot replace this argument: asymptotic bounds concern an unbounded tail.

Likewise, to refute <span class="math-inline">f∈Ω(g)</span>, for every proposed <span class="math-inline">c,n<sub>0</sub></span> find a sufficiently large <span class="math-inline">n</span> with <span class="math-inline">f(n)&lt;cg(n)</span>. To refute a Θ claim it is enough to refute either its O or its Ω half. A single exceptional input does not refute an eventual claim; infinitely many *unbounded* counterexamples can.

### 3.3 The ratio method and its exact limitations

When <span class="math-inline">g(n)&gt;0</span> eventually, inspect <span class="math-inline">r(n)=f(n)/g(n)</span>. If <span class="math-inline">r(n)→0</span>, then <span class="math-inline">f∈o(g)</span>. If <span class="math-inline">r(n)→L</span> for finite <span class="math-inline">L&gt;0</span>, choose <span class="math-inline">ε=L/2</span> in the limit definition to obtain <span class="math-inline">L/2≤r(n)≤3L/2</span> eventually; hence <span class="math-inline">f∈Θ(g)</span>. If <span class="math-inline">r(n)→∞</span>, then <span class="math-inline">f∈ω(g)</span>. If the limit does not exist, **return to eventual upper and lower envelopes**. A limit is a sufficient classification shortcut, not a necessary condition for Θ.

For example, <span class="math-inline">f(n)=n(2+sin n)</span> has <span class="math-inline">1≤f(n)/n≤3</span> for every positive integer, so <span class="math-inline">f∈Θ(n)</span>. The ratio has no limit: the integer sequence <span class="math-inline">sin n</span> does not converge. One can avoid needing that nonconvergence fact by using the explicitly alternating <span class="math-inline">f(n)=n</span> for even <span class="math-inline">n</span> and <span class="math-inline">f(n)=3n</span> for odd <span class="math-inline">n</span>; the ratio alternates exactly between <span class="math-inline">1</span> and <span class="math-inline">3</span>, while the Θ witnesses remain <span class="math-inline">1,3,1</span>. Conversely, a ratio whose liminf is zero and limsup infinite can defeat both O and Ω, as Problem 9 demonstrates.

### 3.4 Leading terms, cancellations, and discrete transformations

For a nonzero real polynomial <span class="math-inline">p(n)=a<sub>d</sub>n<sup>d</sup>+⋯+a<sub>0</sub></span> with **positive leading coefficient** <span class="math-inline">a<sub>d</sub></span>, divide by <span class="math-inline">n<sup>d</sup></span>: the ratio tends to <span class="math-inline">a<sub>d</sub>&gt;0</span>, so <span class="math-inline">p∈Θ(n<sup>d</sup>)</span>. If leading terms cancel, simplify the whole expression *before* reading a degree: <span class="math-inline">(n²+n)−(n²−5n)=6n</span>, not <span class="math-inline">Θ(n²)</span>. Do not apply the “largest term wins” rule to a difference of nonnegative terms without controlling cancellation.

For <span class="math-inline">n≥2</span>, <span class="math-inline">n/2≤⌊n/2⌋≤n/2</span> is false for odd <span class="math-inline">n</span>; a correct robust pair is <span class="math-inline">n/4≤⌊n/2⌋≤n/2</span>. Thus <span class="math-inline">⌊n/2⌋∈Θ(n)</span>. More sharply, <span class="math-inline">⌊n/2⌋=(n/2)+O(1)</span> using the signed-error convention. Shifts by a fixed constant usually preserve polynomial and logarithmic Θ classes, but not asymptotic equivalence for every family: <span class="math-inline">2<sup>n+1</sup>=2·2<sup>n</sup></span> is Θ-equivalent to <span class="math-inline">2<sup>n</sup></span>, while their ratio is <span class="math-inline">2</span>, not <span class="math-inline">1</span>.

## 4. Growth classes, with proofs and conditions

### 4.1 Logarithms and changes of base

For fixed bases <span class="math-inline">a,b&gt;1</span>, the change-of-base identity is <span class="math-inline">log<sub>a</sub>n=(log<sub>b</sub>n)/(log<sub>b</sub>a)</span>. The denominator is a positive **constant**, hence <span class="math-inline">log<sub>a</sub>n∈Θ(log<sub>b</sub>n)</span>. This does **not** license discarding a logarithm whose base depends on <span class="math-inline">n</span>: <span class="math-inline">log<sub>n</sub>n=1</span> for <span class="math-inline">n&gt;1</span>, whereas <span class="math-inline">log₂n</span> grows without bound. Also distinguish <span class="math-inline">log(n<sup>k</sup>)=k log n</span> from <span class="math-inline">(log n)<sup>k</sup></span>, which is generally a different class. We write <span class="math-inline">ln</span> for the natural logarithm and use base <span class="math-inline">2</span> when a numerical binary size is intended.

For fixed <span class="math-inline">k&gt;0</span> and <span class="math-inline">ε&gt;0</span>, <span class="math-inline">(ln n)<sup>k</sup>∈o(n<sup>ε</sup>)</span>. Put <span class="math-inline">t=ln n</span>, so the ratio becomes <span class="math-inline">t<sup>k</sup>e<sup>−εt</sup></span>. Choose an integer <span class="math-inline">m&gt;k</span>. The exponential series gives <span class="math-inline">e<sup>εt</sup>≥(εt)<sup>m</sup>/m!</span> for <span class="math-inline">t&gt;0</span>. Therefore

<div class="formula-block">0 ≤ t<sup>k</sup>/e<sup>εt</sup> ≤ (m!/ε<sup>m</sup>)t<sup>k−m</sup> → 0.</div>

This proof covers every fixed real <span class="math-inline">k&gt;0</span>, since <span class="math-inline">m</span> can be a larger integer. It also implies <span class="math-inline">log log n∈o(log n)</span> by applying the same fact to <span class="math-inline">t=log n</span> and a positive fractional exponent. The statement is about a fixed <span class="math-inline">k</span>; if <span class="math-inline">k=k(n)</span> increases with input size, redo the analysis.

### 4.2 Powers and exponentials

For real constants <span class="math-inline">a&lt;b</span> and positive <span class="math-inline">n</span>, <span class="math-inline">n<sup>a</sup>/n<sup>b</sup>=n<sup>a−b</sup>→0</span>. For fixed bases <span class="math-inline">1&lt;A&lt;B</span>, <span class="math-inline">A<sup>n</sup>/B<sup>n</sup>=(A/B)<sup>n</sup>→0</span>. More generally, for fixed real <span class="math-inline">a</span> and <span class="math-inline">B&gt;1</span>, <span class="math-inline">n<sup>a</sup>∈o(B<sup>n</sup>)</span>. If <span class="math-inline">a&gt;0</span>, write <span class="math-inline">B<sup>n</sup>=e<sup>n ln B</sup></span> and reuse the exponential-versus-power argument with <span class="math-inline">t=n</span>; for <span class="math-inline">a≤0</span> it is immediate from <span class="math-inline">n<sup>a</sup>≤1</span> for <span class="math-inline">n≥1</span>.

A fixed multiplicative factor outside an exponential disappears inside Θ: <span class="math-inline">7·2<sup>n</sup>∈Θ(2<sup>n</sup>)</span>. A change in its exponent's coefficient generally does not: <span class="math-inline">2<sup>2n</sup>=4<sup>n</sup>∉Θ(2<sup>n</sup>)</span>, since the ratio is <span class="math-inline">2<sup>n</sup>→∞</span>. A fixed shift in the exponent does preserve Θ: <span class="math-inline">B<sup>n+c</sup>=B<sup>c</sup>B<sup>n</sup></span>, with <span class="math-inline">B<sup>c</sup></span> a constant. Parentheses matter: <span class="math-inline">2<sup>n²</sup></span>, <span class="math-inline">(2<sup>n</sup>)²=2<sup>2n</sup></span>, and <span class="math-inline">2n²</span> have different growth rates.

### 4.3 Factorials and variable exponents

For <span class="math-inline">n≥2</span>, <span class="math-inline">n!=1·2·⋯·n≤n<sup>n</sup></span>. At least the last <span class="math-inline">⌈n/2⌉</span> factors are at least <span class="math-inline">n/2</span>, giving <span class="math-inline">n!≥(n/2)<sup>⌈n/2⌉</sup></span>. Taking logarithms yields an upper bound <span class="math-inline">ln(n!)≤n ln n</span> and a lower bound <span class="math-inline">ln(n!)≥⌈n/2⌉ln(n/2)</span>. For <span class="math-inline">n≥4</span>, <span class="math-inline">ln(n/2)≥(ln n)/2</span>, so <span class="math-inline">ln(n!)≥(n/4)ln n</span>. Hence <span class="math-inline">ln(n!)∈Θ(n ln n)</span>. This elementary sandwich establishes the class without invoking Stirling's sharper formula.

For any fixed <span class="math-inline">B&gt;1</span>, compare <span class="math-inline">ln(n!/B<sup>n</sup>)=ln(n!)−n ln B</span>. The lower bound above tends to infinity, so <span class="math-inline">B<sup>n</sup>∈o(n!)</span>. Meanwhile <span class="math-inline">n!/n<sup>n</sup>→0</span>: the first <span class="math-inline">⌊n/2⌋</span> factors of <span class="math-inline">n!/n<sup>n</sup>=∏<sub>j=1</sub><sup>n</sup>(j/n)</span> are at most <span class="math-inline">1/2</span>, while the rest are at most <span class="math-inline">1</span>, so the product is at most <span class="math-inline">2<sup>−⌊n/2⌋</sup></span>. Consequently, for fixed positive <span class="math-inline">ε,k</span> and fixed <span class="math-inline">B&gt;1</span>, a useful chain is

<div class="formula-block">1 ≪ log log n ≪ (log n)<sup>k</sup> ≪ n<sup>ε</sup> ≪ B<sup>n</sup> ≪ n! ≪ n<sup>n</sup>.</div>

Here <span class="math-inline">f≪g</span> abbreviates <span class="math-inline">f∈o(g)</span>, and the first relation assumes the logarithms are defined and grow. The chain is illustrative, not a total ordering of every displayed family for every choice of parameters: placing <span class="math-inline">(log n)<sup>k</sup></span> after <span class="math-inline">log log n</span> requires fixed <span class="math-inline">k&gt;0</span>; the relative positions of different polynomial powers or different exponential bases need their own comparison. A more granular and unambiguous example is shown below. Each symbol means that the preceding function is little-oh of the following one. <div class="formula-block"><span class="math-inline">log log n</span> ≪ <span class="math-inline">log n</span> ≪ <span class="math-inline">n<sup>1/3</sup></span> ≪ <span class="math-inline">n</span> ≪ <span class="math-inline">n log n</span> ≪ <span class="math-inline">n²</span> ≪ <span class="math-inline">2<sup>n</sup></span> ≪ <span class="math-inline">n!</span> ≪ <span class="math-inline">n<sup>n</sup></span></div>

For a nonconstant exponent, taking logarithms is often the safest method: <span class="math-inline">n<sup>log₂ n</sup>=2<sup>(log₂ n)²</sup></span>. Its logarithm grows like <span class="math-inline">(ln n)²</span>, faster than the logarithm of every fixed power <span class="math-inline">n<sup>c</sup></span> but slower than the logarithm of <span class="math-inline">2<sup>n</sup></span>. Thus for every fixed <span class="math-inline">c&gt;0</span>, <span class="math-inline">n<sup>c</sup>∈o(n<sup>log₂ n</sup>)</span> and <span class="math-inline">n<sup>log₂ n</sup>∈o(2<sup>n</sup>)</span>. The notation <span class="math-inline">2<sup>Θ(n)</sup></span> denotes a family of exponential-order expressions, not an ordinary algebraic value whose Θ can be canceled from an exponent.

## 5. Algebraic rules and the traps in applying them

### 5.1 Sum, maximum, product, and transitivity

Suppose <span class="math-inline">f<sub>1</sub>∈O(g<sub>1</sub>)</span> and <span class="math-inline">f<sub>2</sub>∈O(g<sub>2</sub>)</span>, with every function eventually nonnegative. Beyond the larger of the two thresholds, <span class="math-inline">f<sub>1</sub>+f<sub>2</sub>≤c<sub>1</sub>g<sub>1</sub>+c<sub>2</sub>g<sub>2</sub>≤(c<sub>1</sub>+c<sub>2</sub>)max(g<sub>1</sub>,g<sub>2</sub>)</span>. Thus <span class="math-inline">f<sub>1</sub>+f<sub>2</sub>∈O(max(g<sub>1</sub>,g<sub>2</sub>))</span>. If both summands are nonnegative, <span class="math-inline">max(f<sub>1</sub>,f<sub>2</sub>)≤f<sub>1</sub>+f<sub>2</sub>≤2max(f<sub>1</sub>,f<sub>2</sub>)</span>; their sum and maximum are Θ-equivalent. This is the valid “largest term wins” principle for positive costs. Cancellation in a difference is outside its hypotheses.

Multiplication follows from multiplying two nonnegative witness inequalities: <span class="math-inline">f<sub>1</sub>f<sub>2</sub>≤c<sub>1</sub>c<sub>2</sub>g<sub>1</sub>g<sub>2</sub></span>, so the product is an O bound. If both original relations are Θ, multiply their lower and upper witnesses to get a Θ product. Transitivity also uses shared eventual thresholds: if <span class="math-inline">f≤c<sub>1</sub>g</span> eventually and <span class="math-inline">g≤c<sub>2</sub>h</span> eventually, then <span class="math-inline">f≤c<sub>1</sub>c<sub>2</sub>h</span> beyond their maximum threshold. Reflexivity <span class="math-inline">f∈Θ(f)</span> holds for an eventually nonnegative <span class="math-inline">f</span>, including the zero function. For fixed <span class="math-inline">c&gt;0</span>, <span class="math-inline">cf∈Θ(f)</span>.

These rules do not prove a **lower** bound for a sum from an upper bound on one summand. They also do not allow subtraction: <span class="math-inline">n²+n</span> and <span class="math-inline">n²−n</span> are each Θ of <span class="math-inline">n²</span>, but their difference is <span class="math-inline">2n</span>. If <span class="math-inline">f,g&gt;0</span> eventually and <span class="math-inline">f∈Θ(g)</span>, dividing the two-sided witnesses gives <span class="math-inline">1/f∈Θ(1/g)</span>. A mere <span class="math-inline">f∈O(g)</span> gives a reciprocal **lower** bound when <span class="math-inline">f&gt;0</span>, not necessarily reciprocal Θ; for example <span class="math-inline">1∈O(n)</span> but <span class="math-inline">1∉Θ(1/n)</span> after reciprocation.

### 5.2 Composition, logarithms of costs, and nonmonotone functions

If <span class="math-inline">f∈O(g)</span> and <span class="math-inline">h</span> maps large inputs to values tending to infinity, then <span class="math-inline">f(h(n))∈O(g(h(n)))</span>, assuming <span class="math-inline">h(n)</span> lies in the domain. The condition matters: if <span class="math-inline">h(n)=1</span> forever, the eventual part of the original bound tells us nothing about the composed values. If <span class="math-inline">f∈Θ(g)</span>, the same condition preserves Θ. An arbitrary outer function cannot be applied to both sides of an O relation: from <span class="math-inline">f≤cg</span> one cannot conclude <span class="math-inline">2<sup>f</sup>∈O(2<sup>g</sup>)</span>. Take <span class="math-inline">f=2n</span>, <span class="math-inline">g=n</span>; <span class="math-inline">f∈Θ(g)</span>, but <span class="math-inline">2<sup>2n</sup>∉O(2<sup>n</sup>)</span>. Even a monotone outer operation may amplify a constant factor into a changing one.

If positive <span class="math-inline">f∈Θ(g)</span> and both grow beyond <span class="math-inline">1</span>, logarithms have an **additive** constant difference, since <span class="math-inline">ln c<sub>1</sub>+ln g≤ln f≤ln c<sub>2</sub>+ln g</span>. If <span class="math-inline">ln g→∞</span>, then <span class="math-inline">ln f∼ln g</span>. If <span class="math-inline">g</span> tends to <span class="math-inline">1</span>, logarithmic comparison can break: <span class="math-inline">f=2</span>, <span class="math-inline">g=1</span> are Θ-equivalent, yet <span class="math-inline">ln f</span> is positive and <span class="math-inline">ln g=0</span>. State positivity and growth before taking logarithms of a complexity bound.

### 5.3 Two or more independent size parameters

For functions of <span class="math-inline">n,m</span>, define a claim relative to an **admissible domain** <span class="math-inline">D⊆ℕ²</span>. One precise joint-tail convention says <span class="math-inline">f∈O<sub>D</sub>(g)</span> if constants <span class="math-inline">c,N,M&gt;0</span> exist such that <span class="math-inline">0≤f(n,m)≤cg(n,m)</span> for every <span class="math-inline">(n,m)∈D</span> with both <span class="math-inline">n≥N</span> and <span class="math-inline">m≥M</span>. An alternative one-size measure could use <span class="math-inline">n+m→∞</span>; it is a different convention and can include paths with one parameter fixed. Name the convention before comparing multivariate functions. If a graph has <span class="math-inline">n</span> vertices and <span class="math-inline">m</span> edges, restrictions such as <span class="math-inline">0≤m≤n(n−1)/2</span> for a simple undirected graph belong in <span class="math-inline">D</span>. It is invalid to replace <span class="math-inline">m</span> by <span class="math-inline">n</span> without an assumption that relates them.

On unconstrained positive integer pairs, <span class="math-inline">n+m∉O(n)</span>: choose <span class="math-inline">m=n²</span>, making the ratio <span class="math-inline">1+n</span> unbounded. On the restricted domain <span class="math-inline">m≤3n</span>, <span class="math-inline">n+m≤4n</span>, so <span class="math-inline">n+m∈Θ(n)</span> because it is also at least <span class="math-inline">n</span>. A one-variable hierarchy is not a substitute for checking every allowed growth path.

## 6. Worked problem bank: witnesses, counterexamples, and growth order

Each problem states what must be decided and why each step is valid. “Inspired by” identifies a source's technique; the statement and solution are original. These are instructional worked problems, **not** a request that the student answer a test before completing the first reading.

### Problem 1 — Exhibit both witnesses for a polynomial

**Task.** Establish a tight bound for <span class="math-inline">F(n)=7n³−5n²+12n+9</span>, with explicit constants.

**Solution.** For <span class="math-inline">n≥1</span>, upper-bound each positive term by its cubic counterpart and discard the negative term: <span class="math-inline">F(n)≤(7+12+9)n³=28n³</span>. For the lower bound, <span class="math-inline">7n³−5n²=n²(7n−5)≥2n³</span> because <span class="math-inline">7n−5≥2n</span> for <span class="math-inline">n≥1</span>. The remaining terms are nonnegative. Thus <span class="math-inline">2n³≤F(n)≤28n³</span> for every <span class="math-inline">n≥1</span>. The witnesses <span class="math-inline">c<sub>1</sub>=2,c<sub>2</sub>=28,n<sub>0</sub>=1</span> prove <span class="math-inline">F∈Θ(n³)</span>. A leading-term limit also works, but this construction directly verifies the definition. *Technique inspired by Stanford CS161's polynomial witnesses.*

### Problem 2 — Refute an overoptimistic upper bound

**Task.** Decide whether <span class="math-inline">n³+4n∈O(n² log₂n)</span>.

**Solution.** The ratio is <span class="math-inline">(n³+4n)/(n²log₂n)=n/log₂n+4/(n log₂n)</span> for <span class="math-inline">n&gt;1</span>. The first term tends to infinity because <span class="math-inline">log₂n∈o(n)</span>, proved in §4.1; the second is nonnegative. Hence no fixed upper constant can work, so the membership is false. Directly, for any <span class="math-inline">c</span> choose <span class="math-inline">n</span> so large that <span class="math-inline">n/log₂n&gt;c</span>, then <span class="math-inline">n³+4n&gt;cn²log₂n</span>. This refutes the eventual universal quantifier, not merely a finite sample.

### Problem 3 — Little-oh versus big-oh

**Task.** Classify <span class="math-inline">F(n)=n(log₂n)³</span> relative to <span class="math-inline">G(n)=n<sup>5/4</sup></span>, and explain why a finite positive ratio would change the answer.

**Solution.** Cancel the positive <span class="math-inline">n</span>: <span class="math-inline">F/G=(log₂n)³/n<sup>1/4</sup>→0</span> by the logarithm-versus-power proof, with fixed <span class="math-inline">k=3,ε=1/4</span>. Therefore <span class="math-inline">F∈o(G)</span>, hence also <span class="math-inline">F∈O(G)</span>, but <span class="math-inline">F∉Ω(G)</span>. If instead the ratio tended to a fixed positive number, the lower and upper witness constants would both exist and the correct relation would be Θ rather than little-oh.

### Problem 4 — Fix a broken logarithm-base shortcut

**Task.** Compare <span class="math-inline">F(n)=log<sub>n</sub>n²</span> with <span class="math-inline">G(n)=log₂n</span> on <span class="math-inline">n≥2</span>.

**Solution.** By definition <span class="math-inline">log<sub>n</sub>n²=2</span>. Thus <span class="math-inline">F/G=2/log₂n→0</span>, proving <span class="math-inline">F∈o(G)</span>. The usual assertion that logarithm bases differ only by a constant applies only when those bases are fixed independently of <span class="math-inline">n</span>. Here the base is the variable input size. The exact value <span class="math-inline">2</span> also makes clear that <span class="math-inline">F∈Θ(1)</span>.

### Problem 5 — Exponent position changes everything

**Task.** Order <span class="math-inline">2n³</span>, <span class="math-inline">(2<sup>n</sup>)³</span>, and <span class="math-inline">2<sup>n³</sup></span> by strict asymptotic growth.

**Solution.** The middle expression is <span class="math-inline">2<sup>3n</sup>=8<sup>n</sup></span>. A polynomial is little-oh of a fixed-base exponential, so <span class="math-inline">2n³∈o(8<sup>n</sup>)</span>. For the remaining pair, the ratio <span class="math-inline">8<sup>n</sup>/2<sup>n³</sup>=2<sup>3n−n³</sup>→0</span> because <span class="math-inline">n³−3n→∞</span>. Thus <span class="math-inline">2n³ ≪ (2<sup>n</sup>)³ ≪ 2<sup>n³</sup></span>. Parentheses are essential: the first expression has a constant coefficient, the second has a linear exponent, and the third has a cubic exponent. *Reasoning type informed by MIT 6.006's exponential-comparison exercises.*

### Problem 6 — A binomial coefficient with a fixed lower index

**Task.** For a fixed integer <span class="math-inline">r≥1</span>, find a tight class of <span class="math-inline">C(n,r)</span> as <span class="math-inline">n→∞</span>.

**Solution.** The exact product is <span class="math-inline">C(n,r)=n(n−1)⋯(n−r+1)/r!</span>. Since <span class="math-inline">r</span> is fixed, <span class="math-inline">r!</span> is a constant. Divide by <span class="math-inline">n<sup>r</sup></span>: the ratio is <span class="math-inline">(1/r!)∏<sub>j=0</sub><sup>r−1</sup>(1−j/n)→1/r!&gt;0</span>, because the finite product has <span class="math-inline">r</span> factors, each tending to <span class="math-inline">1</span>. Therefore <span class="math-inline">C(n,r)∈Θ(n<sup>r</sup>)</span>, and more sharply <span class="math-inline">C(n,r)∼n<sup>r</sup>/r!</span>. If <span class="math-inline">r=r(n)</span> grows, this finite-fixed-product proof no longer applies. *Reasoning type informed by MIT 6.006's fixed-index binomial comparison.*

### Problem 7 — Cancellation defeats a naive dominant-term rule

**Task.** Simplify and classify <span class="math-inline">F(n)=(n³+4n²+1)−(n³−2n²+n)</span>.

**Solution.** Expand the subtraction before applying any growth rule: <span class="math-inline">F(n)=6n²−n+1</span>. The leading surviving coefficient is positive, so dividing by <span class="math-inline">n²</span> gives <span class="math-inline">6−1/n+1/n²→6</span>. Thus <span class="math-inline">F∈Θ(n²)</span>, and indeed <span class="math-inline">F∼6n²</span>. Treating each parenthesis as Θ of <span class="math-inline">n³</span> and subtracting Θ classes would lose the cancellation and produce an unjustified answer.

### Problem 8 — A nonexistent limit with a valid tight bound

**Task.** Let <span class="math-inline">F(n)=4n</span> for even <span class="math-inline">n</span> and <span class="math-inline">F(n)=9n</span> for odd <span class="math-inline">n</span>. Classify it relative to <span class="math-inline">n</span> and decide whether it is asymptotically equivalent to <span class="math-inline">6n</span>.

**Solution.** Every positive <span class="math-inline">n</span> satisfies <span class="math-inline">4n≤F(n)≤9n</span>, so <span class="math-inline">F∈Θ(n)</span>. The ratio <span class="math-inline">F(n)/(6n)</span> alternates between <span class="math-inline">2/3</span> and <span class="math-inline">3/2</span>, so it does not tend to <span class="math-inline">1</span>; <span class="math-inline">F∼6n</span> is false. Lack of a ratio limit did not defeat Θ because two global envelopes hold. *Technique informed by CMU's oscillation example and Princeton's limit counterexample.*

### Problem 9 — Functions that are genuinely incomparable

**Task.** Define <span class="math-inline">F(n)=n</span> for even <span class="math-inline">n</span> and <span class="math-inline">F(n)=n³</span> for odd <span class="math-inline">n</span>. Compare <span class="math-inline">F</span> with <span class="math-inline">G(n)=n²</span>.

**Solution.** On even sizes <span class="math-inline">F/G=1/n→0</span>, so no positive constant can lower-bound the ratio on **all** sufficiently large sizes; <span class="math-inline">F∉Ω(G)</span>. On odd sizes <span class="math-inline">F/G=n→∞</span>, so no fixed constant can upper-bound it on all sufficiently large sizes; <span class="math-inline">F∉O(G)</span>. Therefore <span class="math-inline">F∉Θ(G)</span> and neither function is uniformly no faster or no slower under these one-sided relations. This is different from Problem 8, where the oscillation stayed within fixed factors.

### Problem 10 — Take factorial logarithms without overclaiming

**Task.** Classify <span class="math-inline">H(n)=log₂((3n)!)</span> relative to <span class="math-inline">n log₂n</span>, for integer <span class="math-inline">n≥1</span>.

**Solution.** Section 4.3 proved <span class="math-inline">ln(k!)∈Θ(k ln k)</span>. Apply it with <span class="math-inline">k=3n</span>: <span class="math-inline">ln((3n)!)∈Θ(3n ln(3n))</span>. Since <span class="math-inline">ln(3n)=ln n+ln3∈Θ(ln n)</span> for <span class="math-inline">n≥2</span>, this is Θ of <span class="math-inline">n ln n</span>. Dividing by fixed <span class="math-inline">ln2</span> changes only the constant, so <span class="math-inline">H∈Θ(n log₂n)</span>. This does **not** imply <span class="math-inline">(3n)!∈Θ(n!)</span>; factorials themselves are much more sensitive to the argument.

### Problem 11 — Show a factorial ratio is unbounded

**Task.** Decide whether <span class="math-inline">(2n)!∈O(n!)</span> and whether their logarithms have the same Θ class.

**Solution.** The ratio is <span class="math-inline">(2n)!/n!=∏<sub>j=n+1</sub><sup>2n</sup>j≥(n+1)<sup>n</sup>→∞</span>, so <span class="math-inline">(2n)!∉O(n!)</span> and hence cannot be Θ of <span class="math-inline">n!</span>. Yet <span class="math-inline">ln((2n)!)∈Θ(2n ln(2n))=Θ(n ln n)</span> and <span class="math-inline">ln(n!)∈Θ(n ln n)</span>, so the two logarithms are Θ-equivalent. A logarithm compresses a multiplicative gap into an additive one; equal logarithmic Θ classes do not imply equal original Θ classes. *Reasoning type informed by MIT 6.006's factorial comparison.*

### Problem 12 — Compare a variable exponent to both neighboring classes

**Task.** For each fixed <span class="math-inline">c&gt;0</span>, compare <span class="math-inline">n<sup>c</sup></span>, <span class="math-inline">n<sup>log₂n</sup></span>, and <span class="math-inline">2<sup>n</sup></span>.

**Solution.** Take base-two logarithms of ratios, which preserves their order because <span class="math-inline">log₂</span> is strictly increasing. For the first ratio, <span class="math-inline">log₂(n<sup>log₂n</sup>/n<sup>c</sup>)=(log₂n)²−c log₂n→∞</span>; hence <span class="math-inline">n<sup>c</sup>∈o(n<sup>log₂n</sup>)</span>. For the second, <span class="math-inline">log₂(2<sup>n</sup>/n<sup>log₂n</sup>)=n−(log₂n)²→∞</span> by the logarithm-versus-power theorem. Thus <span class="math-inline">n<sup>log₂n</sup>∈o(2<sup>n</sup>)</span>. The middle expression is superpolynomial for every *fixed* degree but subexponential relative to base <span class="math-inline">2</span> in this precise comparison.

### Problem 13 — Floors, ceilings, and fixed shifts

**Task.** Determine the class of <span class="math-inline">F(n)=⌈n/3⌉+⌊√n⌋</span>, and decide whether <span class="math-inline">F∼n/3</span>.

**Solution.** The elementary floor/ceiling inequalities give <span class="math-inline">n/3≤⌈n/3⌉&lt;n/3+1</span> and <span class="math-inline">√n−1&lt;⌊√n⌋≤√n</span>. Therefore <span class="math-inline">n/3≤F(n)&lt;n/3+1+√n</span> for positive integers. Dividing by <span class="math-inline">n/3</span> gives <span class="math-inline">1≤F(n)/(n/3)&lt;1+3/n+3/√n→1</span>. The squeeze theorem proves the sharper statement <span class="math-inline">F∼n/3</span>, which implies <span class="math-inline">F∈Θ(n)</span>. The square-root and rounding errors are genuinely lower order; no exact equality was assumed.

### Problem 14 — A two-parameter path invalidates a one-parameter guess

**Task.** On unconstrained positive integer pairs, decide whether <span class="math-inline">F(n,m)=n²+m</span> belongs to <span class="math-inline">O(n²)</span>. Re-evaluate on <span class="math-inline">D={(n,m):1≤m≤n²}</span>.

**Solution.** On the unconstrained domain choose the allowed path <span class="math-inline">m=n³</span>. Then <span class="math-inline">F/n²=1+n→∞</span>, refuting a universal fixed constant. On <span class="math-inline">D</span>, <span class="math-inline">n²≤F≤2n²</span>, so <span class="math-inline">F∈Θ<sub>D</sub>(n²)</span> with witnesses <span class="math-inline">1,2</span>. The answer changes because the domain changes; a bound on one path cannot establish a multivariate bound over all paths. *Reasoning type informed by Princeton's multiple-parameter slides.*

### Problem 15 — Exposing an invalid subtraction of Θ classes

**Task.** Decide whether the information <span class="math-inline">A∈Θ(n²)</span> and <span class="math-inline">B∈Θ(n²)</span> determines the class of <span class="math-inline">A−B</span>.

**Solution.** It does not. Three examples, all with nonnegative <span class="math-inline">A,B</span>, show why. If <span class="math-inline">A=n²</span> and <span class="math-inline">B=n²</span>, their difference is identically zero. If <span class="math-inline">A=n²+n</span> and <span class="math-inline">B=n²</span>, the difference is <span class="math-inline">n∈Θ(n)</span>. If <span class="math-inline">A=2n²</span> and <span class="math-inline">B=n²</span>, the difference is <span class="math-inline">n²∈Θ(n²)</span>. All three satisfy the same two premises. One must analyze the actual expressions or establish additional information, such as their leading coefficients or a bound on the difference.

### Problem 16 — An algorithm's upper bound and a problem's lower bound

**Task.** A problem has a proved worst-case lower bound <span class="math-inline">Ω(n log n)</span> for every algorithm in a stated comparison model. Algorithm <span class="math-inline">A</span> has a proved worst-case upper bound <span class="math-inline">O(n log n)</span> in the **same** model. What follows? What if its upper bound were only <span class="math-inline">O(n²)</span>?

**Solution.** The universal problem lower bound applies to <span class="math-inline">A</span> in that model. Combined with its upper bound, <span class="math-inline">T<sub>A,worst</sub>∈Θ(n log n)</span>, and no algorithm in that model can have an asymptotically smaller worst-case order than this lower bound. If the only upper bound on <span class="math-inline">A</span> is <span class="math-inline">O(n²)</span>, then we know <span class="math-inline">T<sub>A,worst</sub>∈Ω(n log n)∩O(n²)</span>, but no Θ class follows: <span class="math-inline">n log n</span>, <span class="math-inline">n<sup>3/2</sup></span>, and <span class="math-inline">n²</span> all fit those one-sided constraints. A lower bound on one algorithm alone would not establish a problem lower bound. *Reasoning type informed by CMU's asymptotic optimality discussion.*

### Problem 17 — Composition and exponentiation are different moves

**Task.** Given <span class="math-inline">f(t)=2t</span> and <span class="math-inline">g(t)=t</span>, let <span class="math-inline">h(n)=⌊√n⌋</span>. Compare <span class="math-inline">f∘h</span> with <span class="math-inline">g∘h</span>, then compare <span class="math-inline">2<sup>f(n)</sup></span> with <span class="math-inline">2<sup>g(n)</sup></span>.

**Solution.** Because <span class="math-inline">f(t)=2g(t)</span> for every <span class="math-inline">t</span>, substitution gives <span class="math-inline">f(h(n))=2g(h(n))</span>; hence they are Θ-equivalent. The fact that <span class="math-inline">h(n)→∞</span> also satisfies the general composition condition. Exponentiating the **values** is a different operation: <span class="math-inline">2<sup>f(n)</sup>/2<sup>g(n)</sup>=2<sup>n</sup>→∞</span>. Therefore <span class="math-inline">2<sup>f(n)</sup>∉O(2<sup>g(n)</sup>)</span>. A fixed multiplicative difference in exponents is not a fixed multiplicative difference in exponentials.

### Problem 18 — Tightness, equivalence, and a finite crossover

**Task.** Compare <span class="math-inline">P(n)=400n+20</span> and <span class="math-inline">Q(n)=n²+20</span> both asymptotically and at <span class="math-inline">n=100</span>. Determine whether <span class="math-inline">P∼400n</span>.

**Solution.** <span class="math-inline">P(n)/n=400+20/n→400</span>, so <span class="math-inline">P∈Θ(n)</span>; <span class="math-inline">Q(n)/n²=1+20/n²→1</span>, so <span class="math-inline">Q∈Θ(n²)</span>. Thus <span class="math-inline">P/Q→0</span> and <span class="math-inline">P∈o(Q)</span>. At <span class="math-inline">n=100</span>, however, <span class="math-inline">P(100)=40020</span> while <span class="math-inline">Q(100)=10020</span>; the quadratic expression is smaller there. Their difference is <span class="math-inline">P−Q=400n−n²=n(400−n)</span>, so the positive-size crossover is <span class="math-inline">n=400</span>. Finally, <span class="math-inline">P/(400n)=1+1/(20n)→1</span>, so <span class="math-inline">P∼400n</span>. The fixed numbers describe these symbolic functions only; no hardware performance follows without measuring implementations.

## 7. High-yield review and difficult-question decision rules

This deliberately compact review is a **companion** to the proofs and worked problems, not a replacement for them. Every statement below retains the assumptions that make it usable.

1. **Name the object first.** Identify the input encoding and size parameter, the charged resource, and whether the function is worst-case, average-case, randomized expectation, or a particular instance's cost. A bound without these qualifiers may answer a different question.
2. **Translate the symbol into quantifiers.** For <span class="math-inline">O</span> and <span class="math-inline">Ω</span>, one positive constant and one threshold work for every later size; <span class="math-inline">Θ</span> requires both. A witness may be loose but must be independent of <span class="math-inline">n</span>.
3. **To refute O, defeat every candidate upper constant.** Show an unbounded sequence of sizes with <span class="math-inline">f/g</span> arbitrarily large, assuming <span class="math-inline">g&gt;0</span> there. To refute Ω, show the ratio is arbitrarily close to zero on arbitrarily large sizes. One small counterexample is irrelevant.
4. **Do not replace “eventually every size” with “infinitely many sizes.”** An alternating sequence can be large on one subsequence and tiny on another. Check both parity classes or all other admissible subsequences.
5. **Use the ratio test only when its prerequisites hold.** With <span class="math-inline">g&gt;0</span> eventually, ratio limit zero gives <span class="math-inline">o</span>; finite positive gives <span class="math-inline">Θ</span>; infinity gives <span class="math-inline">ω</span>. If there is no limit, search for fixed upper/lower envelopes before declaring incomparability.
6. **Distinguish tightness from equivalence.** <span class="math-inline">f∼g</span> means ratio one and implies <span class="math-inline">Θ</span>. A ratio tending to <span class="math-inline">7</span> proves Θ but not <span class="math-inline">f∼g</span>.
7. **Little-oh and little-omega are strict.** <span class="math-inline">f∈o(g)</span> asks that <span class="math-inline">f</span> fall below *every* positive multiple of <span class="math-inline">g</span> eventually. <span class="math-inline">f∈ω(g)</span> asks that it exceed every such multiple eventually. Not-Θ alone proves neither.
8. **For polynomials, simplify first.** Cancel terms, identify the highest surviving power, and require a positive leading coefficient for an eventually nonnegative cost. A fixed-degree polynomial with such a coefficient is Θ of that power.
9. **Positive sums have the maximum's growth.** For finitely many eventually nonnegative summands, the sum is Θ of their pointwise maximum. Differences have no corresponding rule because of cancellation.
10. **Fixed logarithm bases are equivalent.** <span class="math-inline">log<sub>a</sub>n∈Θ(log<sub>b</sub>n)</span> when fixed <span class="math-inline">a,b&gt;1</span>. A base involving <span class="math-inline">n</span> requires change-of-base algebra, not this shortcut.
11. **Read exponent placement literally.** <span class="math-inline">c·a<sup>n</sup></span> has the same Θ class as <span class="math-inline">a<sup>n</sup></span> for fixed <span class="math-inline">c&gt;0</span>; <span class="math-inline">a<sup>cn</sup></span> generally has a different class when <span class="math-inline">c≠1</span>. Parenthesized powers must be simplified before comparison.
12. **The fixed-parameter hierarchy is strict.** For fixed positive powers and bases, <span class="math-inline">(log n)<sup>k</sup>∈o(n<sup>ε</sup>)</span> and <span class="math-inline">n<sup>ε</sup>∈o(a<sup>n</sup>)</span> for <span class="math-inline">a&gt;1</span>. Recheck if <span class="math-inline">k,ε,a</span> depend on <span class="math-inline">n</span>.
13. **Factorial logarithms hide major multiplicative gaps.** <span class="math-inline">log(n!)∈Θ(n log n)</span>, yet <span class="math-inline">(2n)!/n!→∞</span>. Comparing logarithms alone cannot prove the factorials Θ-equivalent.
14. **Fixed-index binomial coefficients are polynomial.** For fixed <span class="math-inline">r</span>, <span class="math-inline">C(n,r)∼n<sup>r</sup>/r!</span>. If <span class="math-inline">r</span> grows with <span class="math-inline">n</span>, do not keep <span class="math-inline">r!</span> as a constant.
15. **Rounding errors must be bounded.** For real <span class="math-inline">x</span>, <span class="math-inline">x−1&lt;⌊x⌋≤x≤⌈x⌉&lt;x+1</span>. This often gives Θ or ∼ after dividing by an unbounded leading term, but not if the leading term stays bounded.
16. **Composition requires the inner argument to reach the original tail.** If <span class="math-inline">h(n)→∞</span>, then substituting <span class="math-inline">h(n)</span> into a valid eventual inequality preserves the relation. Applying an exponential to both *values* can destroy Θ.
17. **Never invert an upper bound as though it were tight.** If <span class="math-inline">f,g&gt;0</span> and <span class="math-inline">f∈Θ(g)</span>, then <span class="math-inline">1/f∈Θ(1/g)</span>. From <span class="math-inline">f∈O(g)</span> alone, only the reverse one-sided reciprocal inequality follows.
18. **State the domain of every multivariate bound.** On unconstrained pairs <span class="math-inline">(n,m)</span>, test paths such as <span class="math-inline">m=n²</span> or <span class="math-inline">m=n³</span>. Restrictions from the problem may change the answer; state them before simplifying.
19. **Do not confuse algorithm and problem lower bounds.** An algorithm's <span class="math-inline">Ω</span> runtime says that algorithm needs time on its chosen worst-case family. A problem lower bound quantifies over all allowed algorithms in a specified model. Matched problem Ω and algorithm O prove asymptotic optimality **within that model**.
20. **Small-size comparisons are a separate calculation.** <span class="math-inline">O(n)</span> can be slower than <span class="math-inline">O(n²)</span> on relevant finite sizes due to constants. Solve an explicit crossover or benchmark the implementations; the asymptotic theorem remains valid.

### A practical five-step decision procedure

For any unfamiliar comparison: **(i)** identify domain, positivity, fixed versus varying parameters, and exact grouping; **(ii)** simplify algebraically, including cancellation and log identities; **(iii)** try a ratio or constructive witnesses; **(iv)** if a ratio does not settle the matter, examine upper/lower envelopes and subsequences; **(v)** state the strongest justified relation, its assumptions, and what the evidence does *not* establish. This procedure handles all eighteen worked patterns, including the cases where no single growth order exists.
