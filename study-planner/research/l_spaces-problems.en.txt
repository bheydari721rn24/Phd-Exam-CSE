@@ 1|Closure through a homogeneous equation|Original problem|closure-line
Determine a basis and dimension of $W=\{(x,y,z)\in\mathbb R^3:x+2y-z=0\}$, and prove that it is a subspace.
---SOLUTION---
The zero vector satisfies the equation. If $u,v$ satisfy it, its left side evaluated at $au+bv$ is $a$ times the left side at $u$ plus $b$ times the left side at $v$, hence zero. This proves closure for arbitrary real scalars. Solving for $x$ gives $(-2y+z,y,z)=y(-2,1,0)+z(1,0,1)$. The two displayed vectors span every solution. In a zero combination, the second and third coordinates force the two coefficients to vanish, so they are independent. They form a basis and the dimension is two. The ambient space still has dimension three; it is the one independent homogeneous constraint that removes a freedom.
---RULE---
A nonzero homogeneous scalar constraint on $\mathbb R^n$ leaves dimension $n-1$. For $x+2y-z=0$, the basis $(-2,1,0),(1,0,1)$ proves dimension two; checking the free coordinates proves independence.

@@ 2|An affine plane is not a subspace|Original problem|affine-failure
For $H=\{(x,y,z):x+2y-z=3\}$, decide subspace status and describe all points relative to one base point.
---SOLUTION---
Zero fails the equation, so $H$ is not a vector subspace with ordinary operations. The point $x_0=(3,0,0)$ belongs to $H$. Subtracting this point from any solution gives a vector satisfying $x+2y-z=0$, and adding any such homogeneous solution to $x_0$ preserves the right side three. Therefore $H=(3,0,0)+\operatorname{span}\{(-2,1,0),(1,0,1)\}$. It has affine dimension two. There is no basis of $H$ in the ordinary vector-space sense. Describing it using a point and two independent directions is the correct affine representation.
---RULE---
A consistent system $Ax=b\ne0$ is an affine translate of $N(A)$, not a subspace. The plane $x+2y-z=3$ has affine dimension two but fails the zero-vector test.

@@ 3|Scaling closure does not give addition closure|Original conceptual problem|union-failure
Is $S=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb R^2:xy=0\}$ a subspace? Determine its span and the dimension of that span.
---SOLUTION---
The set is the union of the two coordinate axes. It contains zero and is closed under scalar multiplication, since $(ax)(ay)=a^2xy=0$. But $(1,0)$ and $(0,1)$ belong to it while their sum $(1,1)$ does not. It is therefore not a subspace and has no vector-space dimension under these operations. Its span does contain both coordinate unit vectors and hence every $(x,y)=x(1,0)+y(0,1)$. Thus its span is the whole plane and has dimension two. One must distinguish the original nonlinear set from the subspace generated by it.
---RULE---
Containing zero and being scaling-closed are insufficient for a subspace. The union of the two axes fails addition, whereas its span is $\mathbb R^2$ of dimension two.

@@ 4|Modified operations with a shifted zero|Original problem
On $\mathbb R^2$ define $u\oplus v=u+v-c$ and $a\odot u=au+(1-a)c$, where $c=(1,2)$. Prove vector-space status and find the zero vector and a basis.
---SOLUTION---
The map $\phi(u)=u-c$ is a bijection to ordinary $\mathbb R^2$. It satisfies $\phi(u\oplus v)=u+v-2c=\phi(u)+\phi(v)$ and $\phi(a\odot u)=a(u-c)=a\phi(u)$. Hence the operations inherit every vector-space axiom. The zero vector is $c$, since $u\oplus c=u$. The additive inverse of $u$ is $2c-u$. A basis is the preimage of the ordinary unit basis: $c+e_1=(2,2)$ and $c+e_2=(1,3)$. A combination in the modified operations corresponds under $\phi$ to the same coefficient combination of $e_1,e_2$, so existence and uniqueness are immediate. Ordinary determinants of the unshifted displayed points do not test this modified structure.
---RULE---
For transported operations, identify the bijection before testing a basis. With $c=(1,2)$ and $\phi(u)=u-c$, the zero is $c$ and a basis is $(2,2),(1,3)$, whose translated vectors are $e_1,e_2$.

@@ 5|A false scalar multiplication axiom|Reconstructed from CMU abstract-space nonexample
Keep ordinary addition on $\mathbb R^2$, but define $a\star(x,y)=(ax,0)$. Which axiom fails, despite closure?
---SOLUTION---
Take $v=(0,1)$. The prescribed operation gives $1\star v=(0,0)$, whereas the scalar identity axiom demands $1\star v=v$. Thus the structure is not a vector space. A successful closure check does not repair a failed identity law. The subset $\{(x,0):x\in\mathbb R\}$ does become an ordinary one-dimensional vector space under the restricted operations, but that is a smaller set and a different assertion from claiming that all of $\mathbb R^2$ works.
---RULE---
When operations are changed, closure alone cannot replace all axioms. The rule $a\star(x,y)=(ax,0)$ fails $1v=v$ at $(0,1)$; only its horizontal-axis restriction has the expected behavior.

@@ 6|Three parameters with only two freedoms|Original problem|redundant-parameters
Find a basis and dimension of $W=\{(a+b,2a+2b,c):a,b,c\in\mathbb R\}$. Describe the coefficient ambiguity.
---SOLUTION---
The parameterization is $(a+b)(1,2,0)+c(0,0,1)$. The two generators are independent because their first and third coordinates isolate the coefficients. They span the set, so the dimension is two. Changing $(a,b,c)$ to $(a+t,b-t,c)$ leaves every output unchanged. This is a one-dimensional parameter ambiguity, not an additional output direction. The parameter map from $\mathbb R^3$ has kernel $\operatorname{span}\{(1,-1,0)\}$ and image dimension two, in agreement with rank-nullity. Merely counting the letters $a,b,c$ would overcount the dimension.
---RULE---
Count independent parameter directions, not parameter names. The map $(a,b,c)\mapsto(a+b,2a+2b,c)$ has a one-dimensional kernel and a two-dimensional image.

@@ 7|The logarithmic vector space|Original advanced conceptual problem
On positive real numbers use $u\oplus v=uv$ and $a\odot u=u^a$. Find a basis, the coordinates of $8$ relative to basis $(2)$, and the additive inverse of $8$.
---SOLUTION---
The logarithm is an operation-preserving bijection to the real line, so this is a one-dimensional real vector space. Any positive number other than one can be a basis vector because its logarithm is nonzero. In particular, every $u>0$ is uniquely $2^c$ with $c=\log u/\log2$. The coordinates of eight are therefore $(3)$. The zero vector is one, and the additive inverse of eight is $1/8$, since $8\oplus(1/8)=1$. The ordinary number minus eight is not an admissible vector. These conclusions use the supplied operations, not ordinary addition.
---RULE---
The zero vector need not be the number zero. In the positive-real space with multiplication as addition, zero is one, basis $(2)$ gives $[8]_{(2)}=3$, and the additive inverse of eight is $1/8$.

@@ 8|A homogeneous-looking nonlinear restriction|Original problem
Let $W=\{p\in P_2(\mathbb R):p(0)p(1)=0\}$. Decide subspace status and find $\operatorname{span}W$.
---SOLUTION---
The polynomials $p=x$ and $q=1-x$ each satisfy the product condition. Their sum is one, for which the product of values at zero and one equals one, not zero. Hence addition fails. Nevertheless $x$, $1-x$, and $x^2$ belong to $W$. The first two generate one and $x$, while $x^2$ supplies the remaining monomial direction. Thus the span is $P_2$, dimension three. The condition is nonlinear in the unknown polynomial even though its right side is zero. Homogeneity under scaling is not sufficient for linearity.
---RULE---
A zero right side does not make a nonlinear constraint a subspace condition. For $p(0)p(1)=0$, $x$ and $1-x$ are admissible but their sum is not; the generated span still equals $P_2$.

@@ 9|Dependent linear constraints|Original problem
Find the dimension and a basis of the solutions in $\mathbb R^4$ of $x_1+x_2+x_3+x_4=0$, $2x_1+2x_2+2x_3+2x_4=0$, and $x_1-x_2=0$.
---SOLUTION---
The second equation is twice the first and adds no restriction. The third gives $x_2=x_1=t$. Set $x_3=s$; the first gives $x_4=-2t-s$. Thus the solution vector is $t(1,1,0,-2)+s(0,0,1,-1)$. The first and third coordinates force both coefficients in a zero combination to vanish, so these vectors are a basis. The dimension is two. The constraint matrix has rank two, giving $4-2=2$, rather than $4-3=1$. Both methods check the independence of restrictions.
---RULE---
Subtract the rank of constraints rather than their raw count. In $\mathbb R^4$, $x_1+x_2+x_3+x_4=0$, twice that same equation, and $x_1=x_2$ impose rank two, leaving dimension two.

@@ 10|A field restriction changes subspace status|Original conceptual problem
Is $W=\{z\in\mathbb C:\operatorname{Im}z=0\}$ a subspace of $\mathbb C$ over $\mathbb R$? Is it a subspace over $\mathbb C$?
---SOLUTION---
Over the reals, $W$ is the real axis. It is nonempty, sums of real numbers remain real, and real scalar multiples remain real. It has real basis $(1)$ and dimension one. Over the complex field, the scalar $i$ applied to the vector one gives $i$, which is outside $W$, so scalar closure fails. This does not contradict the real result: the set of allowed scalars changed. The ambient space itself has real dimension two and complex dimension one.
---RULE---
Subspace status is relative to the scalar field. The real axis is a one-dimensional real subspace of $\mathbb C$, but is not a complex subspace because $i\cdot1$ leaves it.

@@ 11|A dependency certificate from the original columns|Original problem|basis-filter
For $v_1=(1,0)$, $v_2=(0,1)$, and $v_3=(2,3)$, find all coefficient triples producing $(5,7)$ and extract a basis.
---SOLUTION---
The equations are $c_1+2c_3=5$ and $c_2+3c_3=7$. Letting $c_3=t$ gives $(c_1,c_2,c_3)=(5-2t,7-3t,t)$. Their difference direction $(-2,-3,1)$ is a nonzero kernel vector, reflecting $v_3=2v_1+3v_2$. The first two vectors are a basis of the whole plane. The target is one fixed vector, while its coefficients in the redundant three-vector generating list are not unique. In the extracted ordered basis $(v_1,v_2)$ its coordinates are uniquely $(5,7)$.
---RULE---
Redundant generators create coefficient ambiguity. For $(e_1,e_2,(2,3))$, all representations of $(5,7)$ have coefficients $(5-2t,7-3t,t)$; the basis $(e_1,e_2)$ restores uniqueness.

@@ 12|Joint independence versus pairwise tests|Original conceptual problem
Three nonzero vectors in $\mathbb R^2$ are pairwise nonparallel. Can they be independent? Give a certificate and explain the general bound.
---SOLUTION---
They cannot be independent because the ambient dimension is two. The concrete list $(1,0),(0,1),(1,1)$ satisfies a relation with coefficients $(1,1,-1)$, although no pair is parallel. Any $2\times3$ column matrix has at most two pivots and therefore at least one free coefficient in its homogeneous system. That free coefficient produces a dependence. Pairwise nonparallelity only establishes independence of each pair, not of the full list.
---RULE---
Pairwise independence does not imply joint independence. Three vectors in $\mathbb R^2$ are always dependent; $e_1+e_2-(e_1+e_2)=0$ gives an explicit pairwise-nonparallel example.

@@ 13|Every vector need not be removable|Original conceptual problem
For the dependent list $(e_1,e_2,2e_2)$ in $\mathbb R^2$, determine which members can be removed without changing the span.
---SOLUTION---
The relation $2e_2-(2e_2)=0$ uses only the second and third list positions, so either of those positions can be removed. The remaining $e_1$ and nonzero vertical vector still span the plane. Removing $e_1$ leaves only vertical vectors and reduces the span to a line. Dependence guarantees at least one removable member, not that every member is removable. In a particular dependence relation, only positions with a nonzero coefficient can be solved for using that relation.
---RULE---
A dependence permits solving only for a vector with a nonzero coefficient in the witness. In $(e_1,e_2,2e_2)$, the vertical entries are redundant individually, but removing $e_1$ destroys the horizontal direction.

@@ 14|Independence of parameterized columns|Original problem|parameter-span
Classify the span of $(1,t)$ and $(t,1)$ over $\mathbb R$ for every real $t$.
---SOLUTION---
Their determinant is $1-t^2$. If $t\ne1,-1$, it is nonzero, and the vectors form a basis of $\mathbb R^2$. At $t=1$, both vectors equal $(1,1)$ and the span is that line. At $t=-1$, the second vector is the negative of $(1,-1)$ and the span is that line. Each exceptional case has dimension one because the first coordinate of the first vector is always one. It is incorrect to conclude that a zero determinant makes the span zero; it makes a two-vector basis of the plane impossible.
---RULE---
Split parameter values before dividing by a potential pivot. The span of $(1,t),(t,1)$ has dimension two off $t=\pm1$ and dimension one at both exceptional values.

@@ 15|A rank jump that a generic simplification hides|Original problem

The first two vectors are independent. Their general combination is $(s,t,s+t)$, so the third belongs to their span exactly when $a+b=1$. In that case the dependence is $v_3-av_1-bv_2=0$, and the dimension is two. Otherwise the third lies outside the plane $z=x+y$, giving dimension three and a basis of $\mathbb R^3$. @@ 16|Transformed lists and a coefficient matrix|Original problem
Suppose $u,v,w$ are independent over $\mathbb R$. Are $u+v$, $v+w$, and $w+u$ independent? What changes in characteristic two?
---SOLUTION---
Relative to $(u,v,w)$, the new columns are $(1,1,0)^T,(0,1,1)^T,(1,0,1)^T$. Their determinant is two, so over the reals the new list is independent and is a basis of the original three-dimensional span. More directly, a zero relation gives $a+c=0$, $a+b=0$, and $b+c=0$, hence $2a=0$ and all coefficients vanish over the reals. In characteristic two, summing the three new vectors gives $2u+2v+2w=0$ with nonzero coefficients all one. Their rank is two because the first two are still independent. The field qualification is essential.
---RULE---
An invertible coefficient transformation preserves independence over the stated field. If $u,v,w$ are independent, the list $(u+v,v+w,w+u)$ has coefficient determinant two: it remains independent over $\mathbb R$, but has rank two in characteristic two.

@@ 17|A span equality with explicit recovery|Original proof problem
For arbitrary vectors $u,v$, prove $\operatorname{span}(u,v)=\operatorname{span}(u+v,u-v)$ over $\mathbb R$, and determine whether the statement holds over $\mathbb F_2$.
---SOLUTION---
The two new vectors are combinations of the original ones, so the new span is contained in the old span. Conversely $u=((u+v)+(u-v))/2$ and $v=((u+v)-(u-v))/2$, proving the reverse inclusion over the reals. No independence assumption on $u,v$ is needed for this span identity. Over $\mathbb F_2$, the two new vectors coincide because minus equals plus. If $u=e_1,v=e_2$, their original span is two-dimensional but the new span is a line. Division by two is exactly the invalid step in that field.
---RULE---
Prove span equality by recovering both old generators. The replacement $(u,v)\mapsto(u+v,u-v)$ preserves span when two is invertible; over $\mathbb F_2$ it can collapse a plane to a line.

@@ 19|Function independence from evaluation|Original problem

@@ 27|Nonunique representation count over a finite field|Original hard counting problem

@@ 28|A nested dimension chain|Original problem

Proper finite-dimensional subspace inclusions force strict dimension inequalities. In a chain with endpoint dimensions five and seven and two proper inclusions, the middle dimension must be six.

The first vector of $B$ equals the second vector of $C$, so its $C$ coordinates are $(0,1)^T$. The second vector $(1,-1)$ has $C$ coordinates $(1,-1)^T$, since $(2,0)-(1,1)=(1,-1)$. The reverse matrix is $\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\1&0\end{bmatrix}$, which sends $(1,1)^T$ back to $(2,1)^T$. Basis-vector coordinates belong in columns, and the arrow determines which basis supplies the input coordinates.
---RULE---
The columns of $P_{C\leftarrow B}$ are the $C$ coordinates of the $B$ vectors. For $B=((1,1),(1,-1))$ and $C=((2,0),(1,1))$, $P=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\1&-1\end{bmatrix}$ sends $(2,1)^T$ to $(1,1)^T$; reversing the conversion requires $P^{-1}$.

@@ 32|Coordinates in a proper subspace|Original problem
Let $B=((1,0,1),(0,1,1))$. Find coordinates of $(2,3,5)$ in this basis of its span, and explain why an ordinary inverse of the basis matrix is unavailable.
---SOLUTION---
A combination is $(a,b,a+b)$, so the target has unique coefficients $a=2,b=3$. The third coordinate checks consistency: $5=2+3$. Thus the coordinates are $(2,3)^T$. The basis matrix is $3\times2$, so it has no two-sided ordinary matrix inverse. Its columns are independent and coordinate recovery on its image is unique, but not every vector of $\mathbb R^3$ lies in that image. For example, $(2,3,6)$ is inconsistent. One can solve the rectangular system or choose a suitable left inverse; writing the square-inverse formula without checking shape is invalid.
---RULE---
A basis of a proper subspace may give a rectangular basis matrix. The columns $(1,0,1),(0,1,1)$ recover coordinates $(2,3)$ for $(2,3,5)$, but their $3\times2$ matrix has no ordinary two-sided inverse.

@@ 33|Dual coordinates as rows of the inverse|Original problem
For $B=((1,1),(1,-1))$, find the two dual basis functionals and evaluate them on $(5,-3)$.
---SOLUTION---
The basis matrix is $S=\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{bmatrix}$ with inverse $S^{-1}=\frac12\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{bmatrix}$. Its rows give $\ell_1(x,y)=(x+y)/2$ and $\ell_2(x,y)=(x-y)/2$. Direct evaluation gives $\ell_i(b_j)=\delta_{ij}$, establishing the dual property. On $(5,-3)$ the values are one and four, so $(5,-3)=1(1,1)+4(1,-1)$. These are linear functionals reading coefficients; calling the original basis orthonormal would be wrong because its vectors have squared length two rather than one.
---RULE---
Rows of a square basis matrix inverse read its coordinates. For $((1,1),(1,-1))$, the dual functionals are $(x+y)/2$ and $(x-y)/2$, giving coordinates $(1,4)$ for $(5,-3)$.

@@ 34|A polynomial basis with a triangular coefficient matrix|Original problem|polynomial-main
Show that $(1,1+x,1+x+x^2)$ is a basis of $P_2(\mathbb R)$ and find coordinates of $2+3x+4x^2$.
---SOLUTION---
Its coefficient matrix relative to $(1,x,x^2)$ is upper triangular with unit diagonal, hence determinant one and independent columns. As $P_2$ has dimension three, this list is a basis. Write $a+b(1+x)+c(1+x+x^2)=2+3x+4x^2$. Comparing the quadratic coefficient gives $c=4$; comparing the linear coefficient gives $b+c=3$, so $b=-1$; comparing the constant coefficient gives $a+b+c=2$, so $a=-1$. Coordinates are $(-1,-1,4)^T$. Substitution reconstructs the polynomial exactly. The coefficients in a nonmonomial basis are not simply its monomial coefficients.
---RULE---
Coefficient comparison must use the selected basis. The polynomial $2+3x+4x^2$ has coordinates $(-1,-1,4)$ in $(1,1+x,1+x+x^2)$, whose coefficient matrix has determinant one.

@@ 35|Distinct roots remove independent freedoms|Original problem
Find a basis of $W=\{p\in P_4(\mathbb R):p(0)=p(1)=p(2)=0\}$.
---SOLUTION---
Each polynomial is divisible by $r(x)=x(x-1)(x-2)$ because the three roots are distinct. Since the degree is at most four, the quotient has degree at most one. Thus $p=(a+bx)r(x)$, and a basis is $(r,xr)$. They are independent because a zero combination implies $(a+bx)r=0$ as a polynomial and the nonzero polynomial $r$ cannot annihilate a nonzero polynomial factor. The dimension is two, matching $5-3$. The root count applies to distinct nodes; writing a repeated copy of one evaluation condition would not remove a further freedom.
---RULE---
In $P_n$, $k$ distinct zero evaluations leave dimension $n+1-k$ when $k\le n+1$. In $P_4$ with roots zero, one, and two, a basis is $r,xr$ for $r=x(x-1)(x-2)$.

@@ 36|Repeated roots and derivative conditions|Original problem
Find a basis of the polynomials in $P_4(\mathbb R)$ satisfying $p(1)=p'(1)=0$ and $p(-1)=0$.
---SOLUTION---
The first two conditions make one a root of multiplicity at least two, while the last makes minus one a root. Therefore $p$ is divisible by $r(x)=(x-1)^2(x+1)$ of degree three. The allowed quotient is $a+bx$, so $(r,xr)$ is a basis and the dimension is two. The conditions are independent here: divisibility gives exactly three degrees of restriction in a five-dimensional space. Differentiation and multiplicity are being used over the reals; in positive characteristic, one must verify derivative behavior rather than assume every real argument transfers unchanged.
---RULE---
Over $\mathbb R$, $p(1)=p'(1)=0$ enforces a double root. Together with $p(-1)=0$ in $P_4$, it gives basis $r,xr$ with $r=(x-1)^2(x+1)$ and dimension two.

@@ 37|Dependent evaluation equations|Original problem
In $P_3(\mathbb R)$ impose $p(0)=0$, $p(1)=0$, and $p(0)+2p(1)=0$. Find dimension and basis.
---SOLUTION---
The third condition follows from the first two, so there are only two independent evaluation constraints. Every admissible polynomial is divisible by $x(x-1)$ and has a quotient of degree at most one. Hence $(x(x-1),x^2(x-1))$ is a basis and the dimension is two. The two basis vectors have distinct degrees and are nonzero, proving independence by comparing leading coefficients. Subtracting three from the ambient dimension four would be wrong because the functional restrictions have rank two.
---RULE---
Evaluation constraints must be ranked as functionals. In $P_3$, $p(0)=p(1)=0$ already implies $p(0)+2p(1)=0$; the dimension is two rather than one.

@@ 38|A nonhomogeneous interpolation fiber|Original problem
Describe all $p\in P_3(\mathbb R)$ with $p(0)=1$ and $p(1)=2$, and state whether the solution set is a subspace.
---SOLUTION---
The polynomial $p_0=1+x$ meets both data. The difference $p-p_0$ must vanish at zero and one, so $p=1+x+(a+bx)x(x-1)$. Conversely every such polynomial satisfies the two evaluations. The set is an affine translate of a two-dimensional homogeneous kernel. It is not a subspace because zero fails $p(0)=1$. The two directions $x(x-1)$ and $x^2(x-1)$ describe variation between solutions, but are not a basis of the affine set itself under ordinary operations.
---RULE---
Nonzero interpolation data produce affine solution sets. The constraints $p(0)=1,p(1)=2$ in $P_3$ give $1+x+(a+bx)x(x-1)$, with two free directions and no vector-subspace status.

@@ 39|Integral and derivative constraints|Original hard problem
Find a basis of $W\subseteq P_3(\mathbb R)$ defined by $\int_0^1p(x)\,dx=0$ and $p'(0)=0$.
---SOLUTION---
Write $p=a+bx+cx^2+dx^3$. The derivative condition gives $b=0$. The integral becomes $a+c/3+d/4=0$, so $a=-c/3-d/4$. Thus $p=c(x^2-1/3)+d(x^3-1/4)$, and those two polynomials form a basis. Their quadratic and cubic coefficients isolate the two parameters in a zero combination. Both constraints are homogeneous linear functionals and independent: the derivative reads $b$, while the integral has a nonzero coefficient on $a$. The dimension is $4-2=2$, not a count inferred merely from the appearance of calculus notation.
---RULE---
Linear functionals may involve integrals or derivatives. In $P_3$, zero integral on $[0,1]$ plus $p'(0)=0$ gives basis $x^2-1/3,x^3-1/4$ and dimension two.

@@ 40|All polynomials versus finite-support sequences|Original advanced conceptual problem
Prove that the monomials form a basis of $\mathbb R[x]$, but the coordinate unit sequences do not span all real sequences.
---SOLUTION---
Every polynomial is, by definition, a finite sum of monomials with unique coefficients. Comparing coefficients shows independence of every finite monomial sublist, so the full monomial set is an algebraic basis. For sequences, a finite combination of coordinate unit sequences has only finitely many nonzero entries. The sequence $(1,1,1,\ldots)$ has infinitely many nonzero entries and cannot be such a combination. The unit sequences are independent but span only finite-support sequences. An infinite series of unit sequences is not allowed by the algebraic span definition; adding convergence structure would be a separate problem.
---RULE---
Algebraic span uses finite sums even for infinite generating sets. Monomials span all formal polynomials, while coordinate unit sequences span only finite-support sequences, excluding $(1,1,1,\ldots)$.
