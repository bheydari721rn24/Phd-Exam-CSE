# Adversarial search: written-source comparison and synthesis decisions

Written scopes reviewed during 17 October 2026; final author review completed 18 October 2026. This edition remains a student-unapproved review draft.

## Scope of the comparison

Seven university offerings were located and their accessible in-scope written material examined. This is a bounded candidate comparison, not a claim that every course in the world, every offering of these courses, every assignment, or every diagram has been exhaustively read. Coverage is judged against the declared chapter boundary: finite sequential game modelling, minimax, alpha-beta, exact and approximate values, stochastic opponents, search engineering and introductory game-search extensions. A course's reputation is not used as evidence that a statement in it is correct.

The selection uses complementary contributions rather than adding prestige names. The four primary offerings are Stanford CS221, Berkeley CS188, MIT6.034 and Cornell CS4700. CMU15-281 supplies a fifth course contribution for additional mathematical examples and opponent-model exercises. Harvard and Yale are comparison sources with the limits stated below. Reading a course means reading the listed chapter-relevant written scope; it does not mean the entire semester was consumed.

## Synthesis map

1. **Representation and policy.** Stanford supplies the precise state/control vocabulary; Harvard helps expose missing beginner definitions. The chapter defines a state sufficient for all relevant future rules. A chess board plus side to move alone is not sufficient when castling, en-passant, repetition or move-count rules matter.
2. **Minimax.** Stanford's policy inequalities are expanded into a backward-induction proof. Berkeley and Cornell give concrete backups. The lesson separates the value, a root action, a principal variation and a complete contingent strategy.
3. **Alpha-beta.** Stanford's interval explanation and MIT's distant-ancestor cutoff argument are expressed with an explicit fail-soft return contract. The incoming alpha/beta window is a decision threshold, not necessarily an interval containing the current node's true value. Exact and bounded returns are never conflated.
4. **Complexity.** MIT counts individual plies, whereas Stanford's cited module counts a depth unit as a two-player round. The chapter uses `D` individual plies throughout. It distinguishes leaf evaluations, total node visits, generated successors and stored states, and gives assumptions for every count.
5. **Approximation.** Berkeley's feature model and MIT's adaptive extensions are combined with an independently proved nonexpansion bound for minimax and expectation. Searching deeper is not represented as a universal improvement theorem.
6. **Chance and utility.** Stanford supplies opponent-policy expectations; Berkeley covers mixed layers and the possibility of finite-range pruning. Cornell/Yale illustrate why nonlinear changes to terminal utility can reverse expected-utility choices. The chapter derives the exact remaining-probability interval and distinguishes observation before versus after an action.
7. **Extensions.** Berkeley's UCT account and Yale's diagram are checked for reward perspective, unvisited children, update counts and final action selection. Hidden information is taught as a boundary requiring information-consistent strategies, not solved by ordinary full-information minimax. Transposition flags, quiescence termination and iterative-deepening fallback are independently specified.

## Confirmed corrections and safeguards

- Cornell original page15 prints `3^19=19683`. The unrestricted nine-cell configuration count is `3^9=19683`; legal reachable positions are a smaller subset.
- CMU original page39 displays100 beneath the left MIN subtree although its children have values10 and100. That MIN value is10. The independent worked tree uses root10, not100.
- CMU's introductory max-code screenshot initializes to a finite sentinel. A general implementation must use negative infinity or a proved lower bound; otherwise legitimate scores below the sentinel are handled incorrectly.
- Yale original page21's decision line omits the window parameters used by its recursive routines. Chapter code supplies a complete root call and an explicit action-selection policy.
- A chance-node instruction saying “average” means a probability-weighted sum; arithmetic averaging is valid only for equal probabilities.
- Strictly increasing utility transformations preserve deterministic min/max decisions. Merely nondecreasing transformations can merge formerly unequal actions. Positive affine transformations preserve expected-utility comparisons; arbitrary nonlinear increasing transformations need not.
- Stanford's active module counts depth in rounds; Berkeley/MIT often count individual plies. A formula with exponent `2d` is not a contradiction after normalizing definitions.
- Infinite win/loss utilities, useful for an ordinal deterministic illustration, are not used in finite expected-utility calculations. Expressions such as zero times infinity or expectations mixing both infinities are undefined. The chapter uses bounded terminal utilities for probabilistic models.
- “All values on an optimal path are equal” applies to deterministic exact minimax backups along selected optimizing children. It must not be generalized to expectation nodes or to an arbitrary root-value leaf path whose intermediate choices are not optimal.
- A bound return equal to the root incumbent is not evidence that the corresponding move is tied for best. This distinction is checked by a concrete counterexample in the preliminary numerical oracle.
- No historical AlphaGo date or source claim that Go programs are weak is copied as current background. Neither a source index nor an acquired unread file is evidence of actual substantive reading.
