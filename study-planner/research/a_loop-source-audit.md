## Cross-source decisions and checks

1. An operation count is not a runtime theorem until the input measure and primitive-cost convention are fixed. MIT and Princeton both distinguish constant-time access from linear initialization; Stanford and Berkeley emphasize dependence on input configuration.
2. For a for-loop, count initialization, condition evaluations, updates, and body calls separately. A loop with `q` completed body iterations normally tests a simple header condition `q+1` times; `break`, exceptions, or compound guards alter that accounting.
3. MIT's and Princeton's triangular sums agree: a two-index region is counted by its **valid index tuples**, not by multiplying two maximum loop bounds. The same reasoning yields exact `C(n,3)` counts for strictly ordered triples.
4. Berkeley's geometric-sum guidance is made exact: `1+2+⋯+2^m=2^{m+1}−1`, which is Θ of the final term. Starting a multiplicative loop at zero is a nontermination defect, not a logarithmic loop.
5. Stanford's data-dependent insertion work is reconstructed with an inversion count: the number of successful shifts depends on the input, while the outer overhead remains linear. We do not infer a Θ worst case merely from an O sum without exhibiting a worst-case family.
6. Princeton's harmonic/integral example motivates `H_n=Θ(log n)`, but the chapter uses explicit comparison inequalities and boundary conventions for discrete sums. A continuous integral can estimate growth without giving an exact discrete count.
7. All instructional problems and figures in the chapter are original or independently worded adaptations of these reasoning types. No copyrighted problem statement or diagram is reproduced.

## Remaining limits

Only the specified written portions, not every lecture in each university course, were reviewed. Primitive-cost choices, compiler optimizations, integer overflow, and language semantics can change a concrete implementation's measured behavior. The chapter fixes an abstract model and reports where an exact cost is conditional. No finite source survey or manuscript audit can guarantee every unseen question.
