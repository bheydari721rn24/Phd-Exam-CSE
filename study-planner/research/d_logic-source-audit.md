## Review limits and editorial checks

- The current draft is **not** a finished chapter. The audit does not yet establish a multi-tens-page note, exhaustive syllabus coverage, or complete verification of all relevant text within the four core courses.
- The 2024 Berkeley note says in one informal passage that tautological truth must be proved using truth tables. That is a teaching choice, not a mathematical necessity: a valid equivalence chain or sound deduction also suffices. The chapter teaches the broader fact.
- MIT Chapter 1 has now been reviewed from the original PDF, including the finite-checking counterexample, the one-way quantifier-order theorem and countermodel, and the SAT introduction. Plain-text extraction drops overbars and inequality signs: physical pp. 4, 5, 8, 9, 14–17 were checked against page images before equations were used. The printed text on physical p. 17 says that `P AND NOT Q` is true if `P` is true **or** `Q` is false; this is a source typo. Both conditions are required. The chapter uses the correct conjunction semantics.
- Several Berkeley sentences require correction before use: p. 1 treats the irrationality of the square root of 3 as unknown even though it is a known theorem; p. 5 appears to say equivalence follows from a tautological *implication* where a biconditional is needed; p. 8 calls only closed expressions valid first-order formulas, although open formulas are also well formed; p. 9 initially describes one-way entailment as equivalence, before correctly requiring both directions. The chapter uses the precise versions checked against the other courses. These are reasons to retain other sources even where Berkeley provides useful examples.
- Berkeley Concept Check 14 (pp. 12–13) has been independently reformulated and solved as Chapter Problem 8. Its relation-model counterexample was independently checked, rather than copied from the source.
- An exhaustive check over all 512 binary relations on a three-element domain found 169 models of the premise in Problem 8. In all of them conclusions A, B, and D hold; 144 refute conclusion C. This confirms the written proof and countermodel for that finite domain, while the general entailments rest on the symbolic witness argument.
- CMU's alternating “True or false?” slides present false statements before the next slide gives a counterexample. For example, satisfiability of A and B separately does not imply satisfiability of A∧B (take p and ¬p); validity of A∨B does not imply either disjunct valid (take p∨¬p).
- In Stanford's informal terminology, “well-formed sentence” is used where strict syntax distinguishes well-formed *formula* from a *closed sentence*. The chapter should use the strict distinction: a formula with free variables can be well formed, but is not a closed sentence.
- In Stanford's second scope example (physical pp. 6–7), the text mentions the free occurrence of `y` in the left operand and bound occurrence of `y` in the right operand, but omits the free occurrence of `x` in the right operand if the displayed `∀x` scopes only the left operand, as its punctuation suggests. The chapter supplies explicit parentheses and identifies both free variables. This is an interpretation of the printed scope, not a claim that the handout defines an unusual binding convention.
- Past-exam coverage is currently two visually confirmed relevant M.Sc. questions, not an exhaustive archive-wide classification. No unverified answer should be presented as checked. A first course-origin problem has now been selected and verified; more candidate questions still need screening.
