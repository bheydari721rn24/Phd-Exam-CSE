@@ 1|Percept histories are not physical states|Original mathematical problem
An agent has three possible percepts and two possible actions. It acts after each history of length one or two. Count unrestricted deterministic agent functions and memoryless percept policies. Explain the difference.
---SOLUTION---
There are $3$ histories of length one and $3^2=9$ of length two, so a full table has $12$ independent entries. Each entry selects one of two actions, giving $2^{12}=4096$ tables. A memoryless policy assigns one action to each of three current percepts, giving $2^3=8$ policies. The full table can choose different actions after histories $(x,z)$ and $(y,z)$, while the memoryless policy must use the same action for both because their final percept is $z$. This is a syntactic count over all histories; a fixed environment may make some histories unreachable, so distinct tables can behave identically there.
---RULE---
With $p$ percepts, $a$ actions and decisions after lengths $1$ through $H$, unrestricted tables number $a^{\sum_{t=1}^H p^t}$; memoryless policies number $a^p$. For $p=3,a=2,H=2$, the counts are $4096$ and $8$. Specify whether unreachable histories are counted.
@@ 2|The empty-history convention|Original mathematical problem
There are two percepts, three actions and a horizon of two percepts. How does permitting an action before any percept change the agent-function count?
---SOLUTION---
Without an initial action, table entries correspond to $2+4=6$ nonempty histories, so there are $3^6=729$ functions. If an action is also assigned to the empty history, one additional entry appears. It can independently choose one of three actions, giving $3^7=2187$. The factor is three, not a factor of two or an extra history at every depth. This convention does not change the environment's physical state count; it changes the domain on which the behavioural function is specified.
---RULE---
An empty-history decision adds exactly one table entry. With two percepts, three actions and nonempty lengths through two, the count changes from $3^6$ to $3^7$ when an initial action is included.
@@ 3|One percept does not eliminate time dependence|Original mathematical problem
An agent always observes the same percept and chooses from four actions over five decisions. Count arbitrary history-based functions on these decision histories and stationary memoryless policies.
---SOLUTION---
There is exactly one history of each length, so the five decision histories are distinct by length even though every symbol is identical. Each can receive any of four actions, producing $4^5=1024$ functions. A stationary memoryless policy sees only the current symbol, not its occurrence count, and must select the same action each time. It has four possibilities. A controller with a clock can implement a nonstationary sequence, but that clock is internal state and is not part of a stationary current-percept-only rule.
---RULE---
For one percept and $H$ decisions, arbitrary history-based tables number $a^H$, whereas stationary memoryless rules number $a$. A constant percept does not erase history length; with $a=4,H=5$, the counts are $1024$ and $4$.
@@ 4|Reachable histories versus all tables|Original conceptual and counting problem
Two percepts are syntactically possible, but a deterministic environment always emits X on both of two decisions regardless of action. With three actions, count full tables on nonempty histories through length two and distinct behaviours in this environment.
---SOLUTION---
The full domain contains $2+4=6$ histories and therefore $3^6=729$ tables. Only X and XX are reachable. Behaviour in this environment is specified by the action on X and the action on XX, giving $3^2=9$ behaviours. Each behaviour is implemented by $3^4=81$ tables differing only at unreachable histories. The counts satisfy $9\cdot81=729$. Reachability is independent of action here by explicit assumption; in a general environment one must compute reachability under each policy before using this simplification.
---RULE---
Unreachable table entries do not distinguish behaviour in a fixed environment. If only X and XX occur, three actions give $9$ reachable behaviours even though a two-percept full table through depth two has $729$ assignments.
@@ 5|Contingent policy count|Original mathematical problem
A two-stage decision has two first actions, three possible observations after either first action, and four second actions after each observation. Count full deterministic contingent policies and fixed action sequences.
---SOLUTION---
Choose the first action in two ways. For that chosen action, select one of four second actions separately for each of three possible observations, giving $4^3$ continuations. There are $2\cdot4^3=128$ contingent policies. A fixed sequence ignores the observation, so it has only $2\cdot4=8$ possibilities. This counts executable policies from the given start, not a table assigning unused continuations after the first action that was not chosen. Such a larger table would contain behaviourally irrelevant entries and would have a different count.
---RULE---
From a fixed start with $a$ first choices, $p$ possible subsequent observations and $b$ second choices, executable depth-two policies number $ab^p$, while fixed sequences number $ab$. For $(a,p,b)=(2,3,4)$, these are $128$ and $8$.
@@ 6|Counting a specified finite controller|Original mathematical problem
A deterministic Moore controller has two labelled internal states, three percepts and two actions. It updates state from the old internal state and current percept, then outputs the action assigned to the new state. Count syntactic controllers, including the initial internal state.
---SOLUTION---
The update table has $2\cdot3=6$ entries, each selecting one of two internal states, giving $2^6$ possibilities. The action table has two entries, each selecting one of two actions, giving $2^2$ possibilities. The initial internal state has two choices. Multiply to obtain $2\cdot2^6\cdot2^2=512$. This is a labelled syntactic count: isomorphic state renamings or unreachable internal states can implement the same behaviour. A Mealy controller whose output also depends directly on the percept has another signature and must be counted separately.
---RULE---
Count a controller from its declared function signatures. For a two-state, three-percept Moore controller with two output actions, update choices, output choices and initial-state choices give $2^6\cdot2^2\cdot2=512$ labelled implementations.
@@ 7|Vacuum configurations and belief subsets|Original mathematical problem
A two-room vacuum has a labelled position and two independent dirt bits. Count physical states, nonempty belief subsets, and the physical states compatible with percept (L, clean).
---SOLUTION---
The physical count is $2\cdot2\cdot2=8$. Every subset of these eight states is a syntactically possible set belief, so there are $2^8-1=255$ nonempty subsets. The given percept fixes position L and dirt bit $d_L=0$, leaving $d_R$ free. Exactly two states remain: $(L,0,0)$ and $(L,0,1)$. Neither $255$ nor two is the physical state-space size. Moreover, not all $255$ subsets must be reachable under the particular transition and sensor model.
---RULE---
Separate physical states from belief subsets and observation-compatible states. A two-room vacuum has $8$ physical states and $255$ nonempty syntactic belief subsets; observing (L, clean) leaves exactly $2$ physical possibilities.
@@ 8|Exactly k dirty rooms|Original mathematical problem
A robot can occupy any of six labelled rooms, and exactly two rooms are dirty. Count configurations when occupation of a dirty room is allowed, and when the robot must occupy a clean room.
---SOLUTION---
With unrestricted location, choose the two dirty rooms in $\binom62=15$ ways and then choose any of six locations, giving $90$. Under the clean-location condition, each dirty-room choice leaves four allowed locations, giving $15\cdot4=60$. Equivalently, choose the robot's location first in six ways and select two dirty rooms among the other five: $6\binom52=60$. The equality checks the constraint count. Multiplying $6\cdot2^6$ would include dirt patterns with the wrong number of dirty rooms.
---RULE---
For $n$ labelled rooms and exactly $k$ dirty rooms, unrestricted robot configurations number $n\binom nk$; requiring a clean robot location gives $(n-k)\binom nk=n\binom{n-1}k$. For $n=6,k=2$, the counts are $90$ and $60$.
@@ 9|Constraints can couple choices|Original mathematical problem
An elevator has ten possible stopped floors and one aggregate moving state. At a stopped floor its doors may be open or closed. While moving they must be closed. Count legal states and explain why this is not a complete route-planning state.
---SOLUTION---
There are $10\cdot2=20$ stopped states and one legal moving state, giving $21$. The naive product $11\cdot2=22$ includes the forbidden moving-with-open-doors combination. For route planning, one aggregate moving state generally omits the direction and the segment or destination, so its next floor is not determined. The count is correct for the explicitly declared coarse status model, but it does not establish that the model is sufficient for predicting elevator movement.
---RULE---
Count legal combinations before judging predictive sufficiency. Ten stopped floors with either door state plus a single closed-door moving state give $21$ coarse states; direction and segment may still be required for a Markov movement model.
@@ 10|Labelled robots and collision exclusions|Original mathematical problem
Two labelled robots occupy distinct rooms among four, and each room has an independent dirt bit. Count states. When can the robots be treated as indistinguishable?
---SOLUTION---
Choose the first robot's room in four ways and the second's in three, then choose four dirt bits in $2^4$ ways. The count is $4\cdot3\cdot16=192$. If exchanging robot labels preserves actions, dynamics, sensor roles and performance, unordered occupied-room sets have $\binom42=6$ choices and the quotient count is $6\cdot16=96$. Different batteries, capacities or destinations can invalidate that symmetry. The smaller count is justified by a model invariance, not by the robots merely looking similar.
---RULE---
Two labelled robots in distinct rooms contribute $n(n-1)$ position choices. Replacing this by $\binom n2$ requires complete symmetry under label exchange. With four independent dirt bits and four rooms, the labelled count is $192$, and the symmetric quotient is $96$.
@@ 11|Expected utility despite an unlucky outcome|Original mathematical problem|risk-main
Safe pays 4 certainly. Risky pays 10 with probability 3/5 and −2 otherwise. Under utility equal to the numerical payoff, which action is rational? Does a realized −2 refute that answer?
---SOLUTION---
Compute the risky expectation by weighting both outcomes: $(3/5)10+(2/5)(-2)=6-4/5=26/5$. Safe has expectation $4=20/5$, so risky is better by $6/5$. A realized loss of −2 has probability $2/5$ under the model and is compatible with the optimal decision. To challenge the decision, one would need to challenge the probabilities, utilities, available alternatives or evaluation criterion. Judging the choice using future information the agent did not have confuses rationality with omniscience.
---RULE---
Expected-utility optimality does not imply success in every run. A lottery paying $10$ with probability $3/5$ and $-2$ otherwise has expectation $26/5>4$, although a loss occurs with probability $2/5$.
@@ 12|A threshold different from one half|Original mathematical problem|threshold-main
Action A pays 12 in H and −4 in L. Action B pays 5 in either state. Let $P(H)=p$. Derive the entire optimal-action set, including boundaries.
---SOLUTION---
The expectations are $EU(A)=12p-4(1-p)=16p-4$ and $EU(B)=5$. Their difference is $16p-9$. Thus B is uniquely optimal for $0\le p<9/16$, both are optimal at $p=9/16$, and A is uniquely optimal for $9/16<p\le1$. At $p=0$, A pays −4 and B pays 5; at $p=1$, A pays 12 and B pays 5, checking the inequality direction. Merely testing whether $p$ exceeds one half would choose A incorrectly throughout $(1/2,9/16)$.
---RULE---
For utilities A=$(12,-4)$ and B=$(5,5)$, the exact decision threshold is $p=9/16$. A is best above it, B below it, and both tie at equality; “H is more likely” is not a sufficient condition for choosing A.
@@ 13|A negative coefficient reverses the inequality|Original mathematical problem
A pays $(1,9)$ in states H,L, while B pays $(4,4)$. Find when A is preferred, and explain the boundary case.
---SOLUTION---
Here $EU(A)=p+9(1-p)=9-8p$ and $EU(B)=4$. The comparison $9-8p\ge4$ becomes $-8p\ge-5$. Dividing by a negative number reverses the inequality, giving $p\le5/8$. Both actions tie at $5/8$; A is unique below it and B unique above it. The endpoint check $p=1$ gives A=1 and B=4, confirming that A cannot be the high-p action. The algebraic sign has a concrete interpretation: increasing H probability hurts A.
---RULE---
When comparing A=$(1,9)$ with B=$(4,4)$, $EU(A)-EU(B)=5-8p$. Therefore A is optimal for $p\le5/8$, with equality a tie; division by a negative coefficient reverses the decision inequality.
@@ 14|Weak dominance and zero-probability states|Original mathematical problem
A pays $(7,3)$ and B pays $(7,1)$ in H,L. Is A always strictly better in expectation? Identify all ties.
---SOLUTION---
A is at least as good in both states and strictly better in L. The expectation difference is $2P(L)=2(1-p)$. It is positive for $p<1$, but equals zero when $p=1$. Thus weak statewise dominance implies no lower expectation, not unconditional strict preference. At $p=1$, L has no probability and its payoff difference contributes nothing. If a later observation assigns L positive posterior mass, the strict comparison can reappear.
---RULE---
Weak statewise dominance becomes strict in expectation only when strict improvement occurs on positive-probability states. A=$(7,3)$ and B=$(7,1)$ tie at $P(H)=1$ and differ by $2(1-p)$ otherwise.
@@ 15|Positive affine utility transformation|Original proof problem
Prove that replacing outcome utility $U$ by $3U-7$ preserves expected-utility action rankings. Why does this proof not cover squaring arbitrary utility values?
---SOLUTION---
For each action, expectation is linear, so $E[3U-7]=3E[U]-7$. Subtract the transformed expectations of A and B: the constants cancel and the difference is $3(E[U_A]-E[U_B])$. Multiplication by positive three preserves its sign and every tie. Squaring is nonlinear, so $E[U^2]$ is not generally $(E[U])^2$; it also fails to be increasing over all real utilities. For instance utilities −2 and −1 reverse order after squaring. Even an increasing nonlinear transformation on a restricted domain can change lottery rankings.
---RULE---
The transformation $U'=\alpha U+\beta$ preserves expected-utility rankings for $\alpha>0$ because differences scale by $\alpha$. For $3U-7$, ties and strict preferences are unchanged; nonlinear transformations require a fresh lottery comparison.
@@ 16|Increasing outcome transformations can reverse lotteries|Original mathematical problem
Lottery A pays 0 or 100 equally likely; B pays 40 certainly. Compare expected money and expected square-root utility.
---SOLUTION---
Expected money is $50$ for A and $40$ for B, so a money-linear decision maker chooses A. With $u(x)=\sqrt{x}$, A has expected utility $(0+10)/2=5$, while B has $\sqrt{40}$. Since $40>25$, $\sqrt{40}>5$, so B is preferred. The square-root function is increasing on nonnegative outcomes, preserving their certain-outcome order, but it is not affine and changes risk tradeoffs. Neither preference is inconsistent with rationality once the corresponding utility is specified.
---RULE---
Increasing utility transformations preserve certain-outcome order but can change lottery order. The lottery $(0,100)$ with equal probabilities beats a certain $40$ in expected money, but loses under square-root utility because $5<\sqrt{40}$.
@@ 17|Expected utility versus maximin|Original mathematical problem
A pays $(8,-2)$, B pays $(3,3)$, and $P(H)=4/5$. Compare the expected-utility and maximin choices.
---SOLUTION---
The expectation of A is $(4/5)8+(1/5)(-2)=6$, whereas B has expectation 3. Expected utility selects A. The worst payoff of A is −2 and of B is 3, so maximin selects B. Maximin does not use the supplied prior; it protects against the worst state. The conflict is a difference in criterion, not a calculation error. Without probabilities or an alternative criterion, a payoff table alone does not select a unique rational action.
---RULE---
Expected utility and maximin solve different optimization problems. For A=$(8,-2)$, B=$(3,3)$ and $P(H)=4/5$, expectation chooses A with $6>3$, while maximin chooses B with $3>-2$.
@@ 18|Minimax regret is not maximin|Original mathematical problem
Payoffs in H,L are A=$(10,0)$, B=$(6,5)$, C=$(0,8)$. Compute maximum regret and identify the minimax-regret action.
---SOLUTION---
The best payoff in H is 10 and in L is 8. Subtract each payoff from the best in the same state. A has regrets $(0,8)$, B has $(4,3)$ and C has $(10,0)$. Their maximum regrets are 8,4,10, so B uniquely minimizes worst regret. The worst payoffs are 0,5,0, so maximin happens to choose B here too, but for a different calculation. Regret must use statewise best payoffs; subtracting one global maximum 10 from both columns would create the wrong L regrets.
---RULE---

@@ 20|A finite penalty is not a hard constraint|Original mathematical problem
A safe delivery yields 20. An unsafe delivery yields $30-M$. Find the penalty needed to prefer the safe action here, then explain why no fixed finite penalty handles arbitrarily large unsafe benefits.
---SOLUTION---
Safe is at least as good if $20\ge30-M$, so $M\ge10$; it is uniquely better if $M>10$. At $M=10$ the actions tie, so a policy may still choose unsafe unless the tie rule excludes it. If the unsafe benefit can be any $B$, its penalized value is $B-M$. For any finite M, choose $B>20+M$ and unsafe wins. @@ 21|Known is independent of deterministic|Original conceptual problem
Classify a fair die with a known distribution and a door with an unknown but fixed code along the known/unknown and deterministic/stochastic axes. State the modelling assumptions.
---SOLUTION---
The die model assigns probability $1/6$ to each result under a roll. Its distribution is known, but the transition is stochastic. The door opens exactly when the entered code matches its fixed code. If the code is part of the physical state and the actuator is reliable, the transition is deterministic; the agent initially lacks the parameter. It is therefore unknown to that agent. @@ 27|Partial observability without a memory advantage|Original proof and counterexample problem|common-action

Partial observability need not create a decision disadvantage. If A pays 5 and B pays 1 in both aliased states, A is uniquely optimal for every belief and no memory is needed for this choice.

Information acquisition is justified by net task value. If its total remaining benefit is at most 2 and its cost is 5, net gain is at most −3, so exploration is not rational under that criterion.
@@ 35|Computation has an opportunity cost|Original mathematical problem
A fast program selects an action worth 8 in expectation. A slower program selects an action worth 10, but delay costs 3. Compare net performance and give the general condition for using the slower computation.
---SOLUTION---
The fast program's net utility is 8. The slow program's net utility is $10-3=7$, so the fast program is better by one. In general let the expected improvement from extra computation be $\Delta$ and its total utility cost be c. Extra computation is worthwhile only if $\Delta>c$, with a tie at equality. This comparison must include environmental change during thought, computation cost and any deadline effects. Comparing only the selected action quality would omit part of the task performance.
---RULE---
More computation improves net performance only when its expected decision gain exceeds its total cost. Raising action utility from 8 to 10 at delay cost 3 gives net 7, below the fast program's 8.
@@ 36|Current position omits a key|Original state-modelling problem|key-main
At room X, action Open reaches the goal only if a key was collected earlier. Two histories end at X, one with a key and one without. Show why position alone is not a sufficient state and give a sufficient augmentation for this rule.
---SOLUTION---
Both histories map to the same position X, yet the action's legality or successor differs depending on the key. Therefore no single transition $T(X,\text{Open})$ captures both histories exactly. Use state $(x,k)$ with a key bit $k\in\{0,1\}$, assuming all other future-relevant information is already encoded in x. The two histories become $(X,1)$ and $(X,0)$ and their different action outcomes are well-defined. This is a concrete violation of the Markov/sufficiency requirement, not merely a claim that history is generally useful.
---RULE---
If legality or transitions depend on a previously collected key, position alone is insufficient. The histories ending at X with and without a key must be distinguished, for example by state $(X,k)$.
@@ 37|Time can matter in stationary physics|Original mathematical problem
A project action pays −1 now and 5 at the following decision. A safe action pays 2 now and terminates. With undiscounted finite-horizon reward, compare the choices when one versus two decisions remain.
---SOLUTION---
With one decision remaining, the project's later reward lies outside the horizon, so its return is −1 and safe gives 2; safe wins. With two decisions remaining and the promised later reward guaranteed, project returns $-1+5=4$, exceeding safe's 2. The physical transition rule did not change, but the optimal action depends on remaining horizon. A stationary position-only policy cannot implement both choices at the same physical state. Use a time-indexed policy or augment state with the remaining decision count.
---RULE---
Stationary physical dynamics do not imply a time-independent finite-horizon optimum. A project paying −1 then 5 loses to a certain 2 with one decision left, but wins with two; retain time or horizon in the policy.
@@ 38|Distinct beliefs with identical support|Original mathematical problem

@@ 39|Bayesian conditioning must include the prior|Original mathematical problem|posterior-main
Let $P(H)=3/10$, $P(+\mid H)=4/5$ and $P(+\mid L)=1/5$. Compute the positive-signal probability and the posterior of H. Explain why 4/5 is not the posterior.
---SOLUTION---
The joint positive masses are $(3/10)(4/5)=12/50$ in H and $(7/10)(1/5)=7/50$ in L. Their sum is $19/50$. Normalize the H mass: $P(H\mid+)= (12/50)/(19/50)=12/19$. The likelihood $4/5$ conditions on H and asks how often the signal is positive; the posterior conditions on a positive signal and asks how often H is true. These are different conditional probabilities, with the prior determining how much L contributes to observed positives.
---RULE---
Normalize joint masses, not likelihoods alone. A prior $3/10$ with positive likelihoods $4/5$ in H and $1/5$ in L gives $P(+)=19/50$ and $P(H\mid+)=12/19$, not $4/5$.
@@ 40|The negative observation matters too|Original mathematical problem|posterior-main
Under the binary sensor in Question 39, derive the negative-signal probability and posterior, then verify that posterior averaging recovers the prior.
---SOLUTION---
Negative likelihoods are $1/5$ in H and $4/5$ in L. The H negative mass is $3/50$, the L negative mass is $28/50$, so $P(-)=31/50$ and $P(H\mid-)=3/31$. Average the posteriors with their signal probabilities: $(19/50)(12/19)+(31/50)(3/31)=12/50+3/50=15/50=3/10$. This is total probability applied to H. Averaging $12/19$ and $3/31$ equally would be wrong because the signal outcomes are not equally likely.
---RULE---
Weight posterior beliefs by their signal probabilities. For prior $3/10$ and likelihoods $4/5,1/5$, the negative branch has probability $31/50$ and posterior $3/31$; weighted averaging with the positive branch returns $3/10$.
